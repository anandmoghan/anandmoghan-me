Safety and provenance: watermarks, detectors and fingerprints

Voice cloning needs three controls: a consent step before a voice is created, a mark in every output, and a way to detect or trace a suspect clip. The 2024 to 2026 literature covers the second and third, and it also shows how each can fail. Marks get stripped by ordinary audio processing, detectors learn the mark instead of the fake, and fingerprints drift when a model is fine-tuned.

Evidence quality

Numbers below come from the paper abstracts, as the authors report them. Full-text tables were not checked.

Watermarking methods

Method Where the mark sits Reported
AudioSeal (arXiv 2401.17264) Waveform. Generator and detector are trained jointly with a localisation loss, plus a perceptual loss inspired by auditory masking Localised detection of AI-generated speech down to the sample level; single-pass detector up to two orders of magnitude faster than existing models; robust to real-world manipulations
VoiceMark (arXiv 2505.21568) Speaker-specific latents, so the mark survives zero-shot cloning; voice-cloning-simulated augmentations and a VAD-based loss add robustness Over 95% detection accuracy after zero-shot synthesis, against about 50% for existing methods
NeuMark (arXiv 2609.25719) SpeechTokenizer acoustic tokens: cross-attention spreads a 16-bit message across the residual vector quantiser layers Better robustness to neural-codec resynthesis; supports detection and message recovery; the paper analyses a tension between transparency (measured on the reconstruction) and robustness (measured on the original)
MARC (arXiv 2610.11488) Codec-aware token-cluster space for autoregressive audio generators; a multi-bit payload is embedded by scheduling clusters, then decoded on retokenised audio Average bit extraction accuracy of 97.3% on unmodified watermarked audio (speech, dialogue and music); survives diverse codec attacks and resists overwriting

Reported The MARC authors frame the gap that motivates it: codec processing can change the token sequence recovered from a waveform, and most earlier schemes are zero-bit. A zero-bit mark confirms a watermark is present but cannot identify an individual output. Inference For a product that needs to say which account made a clip, a multi-bit payload is the requirement, and the codec path is the test.

Attacks that strip marks

A study of speech enhancement as an attack (arXiv 2609.29040) adds Gaussian noise and then runs a discriminative or generative enhancement model, as a black-box removal attempt. It tests six watermarks: AudioSeal, WavMark, SilentCipher, Timbre, Perth and AlignMark. The authors report that it outperforms existing neural resynthesis attacks and that generative enhancement, which rebuilds the harmonic regions of speech while denoising, is highly destructive to watermarks. Reported The abstract gives no per-method rates. Its recommendation is that robustness evaluations include speech enhancement.

The watermark shortcut in deepfake detectors

The Watermark Shortcut (arXiv 2606.23335) examines detectors trained when only synthetic speech carries a mark. The detector learns the mark as the cue for "fake", with three consequences: weaker performance on unseen data, marked fakes escaping once the mark is removed, and genuine recordings flagged once they are marked. In a controlled white-box experiment the equal error rate rises from 16% to 75%. A black-box test of a commercial API also showed that marking real speech made it look fake. Reported Training with marks on both real and synthetic speech removes the shortcut, and the authors release a paired clean and marked dataset (WASP).

Tracing a clip across training stages

GenTraceBench (arXiv 2609.21738) asks whether a deepfake fingerprint still works after a TTS model is adapted. It covers five TTS architectures, 16 pre- and post-training variants and 49,728 utterances generated from fixed texts and speaker prompts. The setup trains on the foundation model and tests on the adapted one, over binary detection, closed-set attribution and open-set verification with three forensic backbones. Reported DPO and GRPO mostly preserve the fingerprint, while some SFT and pre-training data changes cause substantial drift. In W2V-BERT verification, multi-shot enrolment lowers the equal error rate for the SFT condition from 44.4% to 11.0%, and the SingNet-only condition stays at or above 45%. This is the safety-side counterpart of the post-training recipes in Preference, RL and distillation.

"I would gate cloning on consent, write a multi-bit mark into every output and test it against codec resynthesis and speech enhancement. I would train deepfake detectors with marks on both real and fake speech so they do not learn the mark as the cue."
