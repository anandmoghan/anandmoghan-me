Audio-visual diffusion

Three threads have converged. Large DiT video backbones now take audio as a condition or generate it jointly. Avatar models bolt audio conditioning and long-video stitching onto those backbones. Causal few-step distillation turns the result into a streamer. The 2026 variation is that the teacher can itself be a joint audio-video model, so the streamer inherits sound and picture together. A fourth thread, listening and reacting to the user, is the Level 2 and 3 gap in Synthesia's taxonomy (see the real-time avatar section).

Real-time numbers in this section are author-reported, often multi-GPU, and not comparable across papers.

Video diffusion backbones

The shared recipe is a 3D causal VAE, a DiT denoiser, and a flow-matching or rectified-flow objective Inference. Models differ in attention sparsity, text encoders, and how causal the VAE is.

Audio-driven human animation

These models take a reference image plus audio and render a talking person. Most reuse a video foundation model (Wan, Hunyuan) and add audio cross-attention Inference.

  • OmniHuman-1 (arXiv 2502.01061): a DiT that scales up data by mixing motion-related conditions into training. OmniHuman-1.5 (arXiv 2508.19209): an MLLM writes a structured textual representation for semantic and emotional guidance; a "Pseudo Last Frame" design mitigates inter-modality conflicts. The authors argue earlier models sync to low-level cues such as audio rhythm and lack understanding of emotion and intent.
  • HunyuanVideo-Avatar (arXiv 2505.20156): an MM-DiT for multi-character, emotion-controllable dialogue video. A face-aware audio adapter isolates each character with a latent-level face mask, so audio is injected per character through cross-attention.
  • FantasyTalking (arXiv 2504.04842): audio-to-video in a pretrained video DiT, then lip-focused refinement. FantasyTalking2 (arXiv 2508.11255): preference optimisation (TLPO) with a reward model, Talking-Critic, and a 410K-pair dataset.
  • MultiTalk (arXiv 2505.22647): multi-person conversation with per-stream audio; Label RoPE (L-RoPE) resolves which audio stream binds to which person. InfiniteTalk (arXiv 2508.14033): sparse-frame dubbing for infinite-length sequences, editing full-body motion rather than only the mouth region.
  • EchoMimicV3 (arXiv 2507.03905): a 1.3B-parameter framework that unifies multi-task and multi-modal human animation.
  • Avatar V (HeyGen, arXiv 2606.13872): conditions on a reference video, attending to its full token sequence, instead of a single photo, so it can capture dynamic traits such as talking rhythm as well as static looks. The authors curate 100M+ training clips from 50M raw videos, generate 1080p with no stated duration limit, and report over 10x acceleration from two-phase distillation Reported.
  • Synthesia Express-2 (4 Sep 2025): Express-Voice, Express-Animate (co-speech motion from audio), Express-Eval and Express-Render (1080p, 30 fps, arbitrary length). Express-Render is distilled to two diffusion steps. The post makes no real-time claim Reported.

Joint audio and video generation

Joint models replace post-hoc lip sync by generating both streams together. Audio quality is still the open question.

Ovi (Character.AI, arXiv 2510.01284) is the clearest open recipe.

  • Twin DiT towers: video initialised from Wan2.2 5B; the audio tower has the same shape (30 blocks, dim 3072) and trains from scratch.
  • Bidirectional audio-video cross-attention in every block.
  • RoPE on both streams, with the audio frequencies scaled to match the coarser video resolution. Video latents span 31 frames while audio latents form 157 tokens.
  • The authors note that dense blockwise fusion costs time per sampling step, and name distillation such as DMD2 as a way to reduce steps.

UniVerse-1 (arXiv 2509.06155) takes the other route. It stitches pretrained experts, Wan2.1 (1.3B) for video and ACE-Step (3.5B) for music, with lightweight cross-modal MLP connectors in corresponding blocks. It was fine-tuned on about 7,600 hours of audio-video data.

JavisDiT (arXiv 2503.23377, ICLR 2026) is a DiT for joint audio-video generation from open-ended text. Its synchronisation comes from a Hierarchical Spatial-Temporal Synchronized Prior (HiST-Sypo) estimator, which extracts global and fine-grained spatio-temporal priors to guide alignment between the two streams. It also introduces JavisBench, 10,140 text-captioned sounding videos, and a synchrony metric for real-world content. The authors report that it significantly outperforms existing methods on quality and synchronisation Reported.

Coupling therefore comes in several patterns: same-shape twin towers with per-block cross-attention (Ovi), an asymmetric pair with a larger video stream (LTX-2, 14B video and 5B audio), a Mixture-of-Experts joint model (MOVA), explicit synchronisation priors (JavisDiT), or pretrained experts joined by connectors (UniVerse-1). Ovi handles the token-rate mismatch with RoPE rescaling.

Joint models are the route the Synthesia post argues for at Level 2: Synthesia says that listening avatars require treating audio and video as a single problem (post). Dubbing is the clearest product use. JUST-DUB-IT (arXiv 2601.22143) adapts a foundation audio-video diffusion model to video-to-video dubbing with a lightweight LoRA, generating translated audio and synchronised facial motion together. For training pairs, it uses the generative model to make clips where the language switches midway, then inpaints face and audio in each half to match the other language. The authors report better visual fidelity, lip sync and robustness than existing dubbing pipelines Reported. The reverse direction, video to speech, is covered by HiCoDiT (arXiv 2604.15923), a hierarchical codec diffusion transformer that conditions lower RVQ levels on lip motion and identity, and higher levels on facial expression for prosody.

Streaming and real-time

The recipe is to distil a bidirectional teacher into a causal few-step student with a KV cache. The teacher is increasingly a joint audio-video model (Ovi, LTX-2), so the student generates sound and picture together. The failure mode is error accumulation over long rollouts (CausVid, Self Forcing and AvatarForcing all discuss it).

Figures are author-reported and use different GPUs (one H100, two H200, five H800), resolutions and definitions of latency. Wan-Streamer's total includes 350 ms of network time; the other rows are not stated to include network time. All figures here come from the papers' own abstracts or text.

Error accumulation has several named causes and fixes. The train-test gap is closed by training on own rollouts (Self Forcing). LiveTalk reports flicker and black frames when Self Forcing meets multimodal conditioning, and repairs them with a better distillation recipe. Identity drift is handled with identity sinks (LiveTalk) and a reference sink (StreamAvatar). Mechanisms are rolling or sink KV caches, on-policy rollouts and distribution matching.

Decoupled Self-Forcing (arXiv 2609.10317) frames two routes: end-to-end audio-conditioned video diffusion (high quality, but only at large scale) against two-stage methods that generate an intermediate motion representation (cheaper, lower fidelity). Its own approach pairs a small causal AR motion generator with a diffusion renderer and reports 15.4 FPS at 1.3 s latency. SoulX-FlashTalk (arXiv 2512.23379) argues that strictly unidirectional attention often compromises visual fidelity.

Listening and reacting

Everything above drives the face from the avatar's own audio. Two 2026 papers add the user. Avatar Forcing (arXiv 2601.00664, CVPR 2026) models live interaction with diffusion forcing: the avatar takes the user's audio and motion and responds to spoken and nonverbal cues such as nods and laughter. The paper names two challenges, real-time causal motion generation and learning expressive reactions without extra labels, and addresses the second with preference optimisation on losing samples made by removing the user conditions. FacePlex (arXiv 2606.30145) generates speech and facial motion together and renders with Gaussian splatting. It predicts a short speech continuation so facial motion is ready before the audio plays, updates as new user audio arrives without seeing future user input, and routes behaviour through a semantic router trained on SemReact, a dataset linking dialogue context, reaction semantics and facial motion. Reported: 122 ms end-to-end avatar interaction Reported.

Metrics and data

  • Lip sync: Sync-C and Sync-D (also LSE-C and LSE-D) come from SyncNet, a pretrained audio-visual embedding network. Sync-D is the average distance between the visual and audio embeddings (lower is better); Sync-C is SyncNet's lip-audio confidence (higher is better) (arXiv 2405.04327). Ovi's data filter uses SyncNet to filter out clips lacking audio-video synchronization (arXiv 2510.01284); UniVerse-1 reports LSE-C (arXiv 2509.06155).
  • Joint-generation benchmarks: JavisBench has 10,140 captioned sounding videos with a proposed synchrony metric (arXiv 2503.23377); VABench scores synchronised audio-video generation on 15 dimensions across text-to-audio-video, image-to-audio-video and stereo tasks, including audio-video synchronisation and lip-speech consistency (arXiv 2512.09299).
  • Conversational benchmark: VideoFDB has 237 dyadic clips from real video calls across 11 nonverbal dynamics, with a taxonomy separating perception from generation and a rubric scored by a language-model judge (arXiv 2605.30256). It reports that models often collapse into generic captioning and largely ignore the video stream, and that cascaded speech-to-avatar systems cannot produce full-duplex nonverbal cues.
  • Identity: UniVerse-1 scores identity as mean DINOv3 feature similarity between the reference image and each generated frame (arXiv 2509.06155).
  • Datasets named in the papers above: HDTF, CelebV-HQ and EMTD (InfiniteTalk), and HDTF, AVSpeech and CelebV-HQ (LiveTalk).
  • Human preference studies (Ovi ran a blind pairwise study with 50 participants) and reward-model judges (Talking-Critic, arXiv 2508.11255) appear in the papers above.
  • Ovi's curation also filters clips on a minimum mean volume and adds captions (arXiv 2510.01284).
Comparability

Inference Papers use different benchmarks, baselines and sync definitions, so cross-paper sync and identity numbers should not be compared.

In the interview, say:

"Audio is moving into the backbone, and streaming is the frontier. The standard fix is distillation plus on-policy training, and multimodal conditioning is where it breaks. In 2026 the teachers became joint audio-video models, and the open gap is avatars that listen, not only talk. Because published sync numbers are not comparable, I would build a shared internal benchmark first."
