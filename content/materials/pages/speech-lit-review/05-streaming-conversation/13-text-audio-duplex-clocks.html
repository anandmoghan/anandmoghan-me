Text rate, audio rate and duplex clocks

Text is slow and audio is fast, so a model that emits both must choose how to align them. This section shows the two alignments and the clock a duplex model adds on top.

Text tokens against audio frames

Text is slow. Moshi puts text on the same 12.5 Hz grid as audio and fills the empty frames with a PAD token. In English conversational speech padding is about 65% of text-stream tokens (arXiv 2410.00037), so about 35% of frames carry a real token, roughly \( 0.35 \times 12.5 \approx 4.4 \) text tokens/s (derived).

Interleaving models drop the shared grid and fix an order instead. GLM-4-Voice (arXiv 2412.02612) alternates 13 text tokens with 26 speech tokens (1:2). At 12.5 Hz, 26 speech tokens last \( 26 / 12.5 = 2.08 \) s, so the text runs at \( 13 / 2.08 = 6.25 \) tokens/s (derived).

Duplex clocks

A duplex model needs one clock that both directions share. In Moshi, every 80 ms step reads the user's 8 audio codes and emits the agent's text token and 8 audio codes. There is no turn state; silence is just tokens. One temporal-transformer pass plus up to 17 depth sub-steps must finish before the next frame is due. Inference If the temporal pass were free, the budget is \( 80 / 17 \approx 4.7 \) ms per sub-step (derived).

The clock can carry more than the agent's own text. Duplex models usually lack a transcript of what the user said, which logging, accessibility and quality monitoring need. One proposal adds a lightweight streaming ASR head in parallel to the agent text head, with minimal extra parameters and no significant architectural change (arXiv 2609.15759). The authors report a streaming average WER of 10.21% on the HuggingFace Open ASR Leaderboard inside the duplex model, and 7.73% when the same architecture is trained as a standalone streaming ASR model.
