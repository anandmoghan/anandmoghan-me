Timing logic: choosing input and output rates

Two rates matter when you design a speech model: how fast audio enters the LLM, and how fast the LLM must emit audio frames. They are set by different forces, and the output rate is the one that fixes your latency floor. The clocks themselves are defined in Rates, frames and clocks; this section covers how to choose them. Numbers are cited to the paper; arithmetic is marked derived, reasoning is marked Inference.

The input path, step by step

  1. 16 kHz waveform, cut into 25 ms windows with a 10 ms hop: 100 Hz mel frames (arXiv 2212.04356).
  2. A Whisper-style encoder has a stride-2 convolution: 50 Hz, 20 ms per frame (arXiv 2212.04356; 50 Hz is derived).
  3. A pooling or adapter stage cuts the rate again, to 10 to 25 Hz before the LLM.

Inference Input can be fast and continuous because the audio already exists. The encoder reads it in parallel with no sampling loop, so a high rate only costs encoder compute. Output must be generated one step at a time, each step conditioned on the last, so every extra frame per second is an extra serial LLM step. The pooling stage exists to move that cost out of the LLM, where each position is expensive.

The output path: what fixes the rate

The output rate is the frame rate of the audio codec (or tokenizer) the LLM predicts. Each frame becomes one id per codebook, so \( \text{tokens/s} = f \times Q \), and the bitrate adds the bits per id:

\[ \text{bitrate} = f \times Q \times \log_2 K \]

Mimi: \( 12.5 \times 8 \times 11 = 1100 \) bps (derived), matching the 1.1 kbps in the paper (arXiv 2410.00037). The paper states frame and stride correspond to 80 ms, and that 8 codebooks at 12.5 Hz give "a sequence length of 100 steps per second", against around 3 to 4 text tokens per second for English.

How to choose the output rate

  • Sequence length and compute. Mimi's 100 steps per second is the figure the paper weighs against 3 to 4 text tokens per second (arXiv 2410.00037). VibeVoice's 7.5 Hz is chosen to cut decoding steps (arXiv 2508.19205).
  • Reconstruction quality. WavTokenizer scores PESQ 1.70 and UTMOS 3.60 at 40 tokens/s against 2.37 and 4.05 at 75 (arXiv 2408.16532, Table 1). Pooling X-Codec 2 to a 25 Hz variant raises UTMOSv2 on seven languages, for example Dutch 2.168 to 2.457 (arXiv 2601.20185).
  • Intelligibility and LM difficulty. SiTok WER is 23.0 at 6.25 Hz, 4.06 at 12.5 Hz and 3.05 at 25 Hz (arXiv 2602.06602). TaDiCodec reaches 6.25 Hz with WER 3.02, but removing the text input at decode causes "significant performance degradation under extremely low token rate" (arXiv 2508.16790). A probing study measures WavTokenizer WER at 11.37% (75 tokens/s) against 24.36% (40 tokens/s) (arXiv 2606.16969, Table 1); it says models below 50 Hz without architectural accommodations degrade, and it attributes its own DAC cliff to a training misconfiguration, so it is not evidence of an intrinsic floor.
  • Text-rate matching. GLM-4-Voice alternates 13 text tokens and 26 speech tokens, a 1:2 ratio chosen so text generation is "consistently faster than speech" (arXiv 2412.02612). Details: text and audio clocks.
  • Reaction granularity. Inference One frame is the smallest unit the model can emit or react to: 80 ms at 12.5 Hz, 40 ms at 25 Hz.

Why 12.5 Hz

Three pressures meet at 12.5 Hz, and a fourth sets the cost.

  • Sequence length and compute. Voxtral cuts its 50 Hz encoder output with a 4x adapter to 12.5 Hz (arXiv 2507.13264). Thirty minutes (1,800 s) at 50 Hz is \( 1800 \times 50 = 90{,}000 \) positions; at 12.5 Hz it is 22,500 (derived).
  • Text-rate matching. Moshi puts English at around 3 to 4 text tokens per second against 100 audio steps per second at 8 codebooks (arXiv 2410.00037). At 12.5 Hz that is about \( 12.5 / 4 \approx 3 \) frames per text token (derived).
  • The quality knee. SiTok WER is 23.0, 4.06 and 3.05 at 6.25, 12.5 and 25 Hz (arXiv 2602.06602). Going from 12.5 to 25 Hz buys little; going to 6.25 Hz costs a lot.
  • Reaction quantum. An 80 ms frame is shorter than the median 151 ms by which human listeners start speaking before the other turn ends in smooth floor transfers (arXiv 2608.25218). Inference So the frame is not the binding constraint on human-like timing.
  • The cost. Pooling discards fine acoustic detail. Kimi-Audio keeps continuous 50 Hz Whisper features alongside its 12.5 Hz discrete semantic tokens (arXiv 2504.18425).

Who emits what, per frame

The LLM does not predict one text token per input speech frame. How text lines up with frames depends on the design:

Design What happens per frame or chunk Source
Encoder + adapter (understanding, ASR) Frames are context only. No per-frame prediction; text is generated afterwards, with no 1:1 alignment Qwen2-Audio (arXiv 2407.10759), Voxtral (arXiv 2507.13264)
Chunked streaming ASR Text tokens are generated per chunk until an end-of-chunk token; 1.28 s chunks SpeechLLM-XL (arXiv 2410.03752)
Time-aligned delayed streams One text-stream token per 80 ms frame, mostly PAD (about 65% in Moshi); DSM's ASR text lags audio by a default 2.5 s Moshi (arXiv 2410.00037), DSM (arXiv 2509.08753)
Interleaved output User speech is context; output alternates 13 text : 26 speech tokens, text leading GLM-4-Voice (arXiv 2412.02612)
Full-duplex dual stream Each frame: read user audio tokens, emit own text token and own audio tokens (K = 17 sub-steps) Moshi (arXiv 2410.00037)

So text and speech are 1:1 per frame only in time-aligned designs, and that is why those designs pad the text stream.

Decoupling the backbone rate from the output rate

If every codebook needed its own backbone step, Q codebooks at 12.5 Hz would force a \( 12.5 \times Q \) Hz backbone. Designs avoid this by letting one backbone step cover many tokens:

  • Moshi: a depth transformer fills K = 2Q + 1 = 17 sub-tokens within each 80 ms frame (arXiv 2410.00037).
  • Qwen3-Omni Talker: a linear head predicts codebook 0 per frame, and an MTP module generates the residual codebooks (arXiv 2509.17765).
  • VITA-Audio: ten audio tokens in a single forward pass, 3 to 5x speed-up (arXiv 2505.03739).
  • MusicGen: a delay pattern shifts codebook k by k-1 steps, so one pass predicts all codebooks (arXiv 2306.05284).
  • Patches: VibeVoice and DiTAR predict a patch per step, see continuous autoregression.
  • DiffuPlex predicts multiple future user and assistant frames in one backbone wake: 1.46x and 1.59x wall-clock speedups (arXiv 2610.12214).

Minimums and rules of thumb

  1. Pool the encoder to 12.5 to 25 Hz before the LLM (arXiv 2507.13264, 2509.17765, 2412.02612, 2504.18425).
  2. Default output rate 12.5 Hz (arXiv 2410.00037, 2412.02612, 2509.08753, 2602.06602); SiTok shows a steep WER penalty below it.
  3. Go to 25 Hz when reconstruction is the bottleneck (arXiv 2412.10117, 2601.20185; SiTok has its best WER at 25 Hz, arXiv 2602.06602).
  4. A lower frame rate is cheaper for the LM (arXiv 2306.06546, 2410.00037).
  5. When interleaving, let text lead speech (arXiv 2412.02612 at 1:2; Moshi's 3 to 4 text tokens/s, arXiv 2410.00037).
  6. With many codebooks, emit several tokens per backbone step (arXiv 2410.00037, 2509.17765, 2505.03739, 2610.12214).
  7. Inference The minimum reaction time is one frame. Practical chunks are larger: Moshi's latency is 160 ms in theory and 200 ms in practice (arXiv 2410.00037); SyncLLM uses chunks of 160 to 240 ms (arXiv 2409.15594).
  8. Inference from the source 6.25 Hz is viable only when the transcript is available at decode time (arXiv 2508.16790, ablation).

The 25 Hz design reacts twice as finely but runs the backbone twice as often (derived). The 12.5 Hz design runs the backbone half as often but adds a depth pass inside each 80 ms deadline. See the per-frame deadline.

In the interview, say:

"I keep five numbers apart. Sample rate is samples per second, like 24 kHz. Feature rate is the 100 Hz mel hop, encoder frame rate is 50 Hz, and the rate into the LLM is pooled to 12.5 Hz. Token rate is frame rate times codebooks, 100 per second for Mimi, and bitrate adds bits per token, 1.1 kbps. A chunk is a batch of frames, so it is a latency choice and not a rate."
