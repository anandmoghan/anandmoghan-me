Ways to model streaming ASR and TTS in speech LLMs

Streaming ASR and streaming TTS each split into a handful of families, and each family answers two questions. For ASR: what sets the latency floor, and who decides when to emit a word? For TTS: does the text arrive complete or incrementally, how far does the decoder look ahead, and how many tokens come out per step? Numbers are the authors' own, on different hardware and test sets, so compare shapes, not digits. Background is in Streaming approaches for ASR, Streaming and low latency, Moshi and Delay and interleaving.

Chunked (SpeechLLM-XL). A decoder-only model reads audio in fixed chunks and emits the chunk's text followed by an end-of-chunk token. Training uses CTC forced alignment and an auxiliary CTC loss weighted 0.5. Chunk length is the latency dial: test clean / other WER is 2.5 / 6.5 at 2.56 s, 2.7 / 6.7 at 1.28 s and 3.1 / 7.8 at 0.32 s (arXiv 2410.03752).

Wait token (Speech ReaLLM). An RNN-T-style blank or wait token is predicted after every input embedding, so the LLM decides per frame whether to emit. Latency is the 240 ms input frame plus 960 ms of encoder right context. The 80M model reaches WER 3.0 / 7.4 on LibriSpeech, with RTF 0.79 under greedy decoding (arXiv 2406.09569).

Transducer with an LLM predictor (Transducer-Llama). The transducer keeps its streaming alignment and uses a Llama as the prediction network. With 160 ms segments and a Llama3-8b predictor, WER is 2.47 / 6.53 against 3.65 / 8.96 for a standard RNN-T. The paper uses a weak-to-strong LM swap and MWER fine-tuning (arXiv 2412.16464).

Delayed stream (Kyutai DSM). Text and audio share a clock and the text is simply delayed, 2.5 s by default for ASR. DSM-ASR scores 1.7 / 4.3 on LibriSpeech clean / other, against 3.3 / 4.7 for streaming Parakeet-TDT-v2, with delay precision of about 300 ms (arXiv 2509.08753). This is the family from Delay and interleaving.

Delay as an input (Voxtral Realtime). A model of about 4.4B parameters (970M encoder plus 3.4B decoder), trained end to end for streaming on top of the DSM framework. The encoder is causal and trained from scratch (970M parameters, 50 Hz, a 750-frame, 15 s sliding window), a 4x MLP adapter brings it to 12.5 Hz, and the 3.4B decoder is initialised from Ministral 3B. The delay is embedded sinusoidally and injected through Ada RMS-Norm in every Transformer block; training samples it from 80 to 2400 ms in 80 ms steps. LibriSpeech clean / other WER is 2.08 / 5.54 at 480 ms and 1.96 / 4.59 at 960 ms, against 1.84 / 3.66 for offline Whisper (arXiv 2602.11298).

Factorized DSM. Removing the word-start token from DSM keeps recognition competitive, and the factorized variant then models "wait" as its own sigmoid, separate from the distribution over the LLM vocabulary. On waiting steps the vocabulary softmax is skipped: 74.70% of steps on the Corpus of Spontaneous Japanese and 66.89% on LibriSpeech. On LibriSpeech at 480 ms, WER is 6.17 (3.66 / 8.68) against 6.52 (3.86 / 9.17) for DSM, and average VRAM at batch size 32 falls from 65.82 to 24.51 GiB (arXiv 2610.04333).

Learned read/write. A lightweight decoder scores each incoming frame, and when the score crosses a threshold the buffered speech and the previous token go to the LLM, which predicts the next token (a MoChA-style policy trained jointly with the LLM and discarded at inference). A minimum-latency loss with weight 0.1 penalises expected latency against HMM forced-alignment boundaries. On AISHELL-2 the average latency fell from 16 to 6 frames of 40 ms (a 62.5% cut) while CER moved from 5.4% to 5.5% (arXiv 2601.22779).

Cache-aware transducer (Nemotron Speech streaming). A 24-layer cache-aware FastConformer-RNNT of about 600M parameters; the attention context is set in 80 ms frames, giving chunk sizes of 0.08, 0.16, 0.56 and 1.12 s with average WER of 8.43, 7.67, 7.07 and 6.93. The model has built-in punctuation and capitalisation, which the WER figures exclude (NVIDIA model card).

CTC only (Granite 5.0 TurboCTC). A 470M encoder-only model of 16 Conformer blocks (hidden size 1024, 16,384 BPE units) trained with CTC loss; 100 Hz log-mel features are stacked to 50 Hz and two stride-2 blocks bring the output to 12.5 Hz. Speed was measured with greedy decoding at an RTFx of 13,715 on one H100 (batch size 128), against 9,229 for the baseline; that checkpoint predates distillation and robust fine-tuning. The final model averages 5.72% WER over seven public English test sets, and the abstract places it on the Open ASR speed-accuracy Pareto frontier at twice the speed of the fastest competitor (arXiv 2609.20104). Inference It sets the speed baseline that LLM-based families trade against, since there is no autoregressive decoder to wait for.

Read/write policies. These wrap an offline model. LocalAgreement takes the longest common prefix of n = 2 successive updates over a buffer of about 30 s trimmed at sentence boundaries, at 3.3 s latency on long-form audio in the abstract (Table 3: 3.62 s at a 1.0 s minimum chunk; arXiv 2307.14743). Simul-Whisper stops decoding at the end of the received audio using cross-attention, with truncation detection (arXiv 2406.10052). Its abstract reports 1.46% average degradation at a 1 s chunk; Table 1 gives 2.34 for Large-v2, so I treat the abstract figure with care.

Unified streaming and offline (Qwen3-ASR). The AuT encoder runs at 12.5 Hz with a flash-attention window of 1 to 8 s, so one model serves both modes. The 1.7B model averages 2.69 offline and 3.33 streaming (2 s chunks in the evaluation); the 0.6B model reaches a time to first token as low as 92 ms with vLLM (arXiv 2601.21337).

Measuring and optimising ASR latency

A delay or chunk size is a design parameter, and papers now argue it is not the latency a user perceives.

The GRPO paper's argument is that alignment-based supervision, as used in delayed streams modeling, forces the model to hold back words it could already emit, so rewarding earlier output recovers latency without architecture changes (arXiv 2609.18333).

End of turn inside the recogniser

Silence timeouts fail because pauses inside a turn can be longer than the gaps between turns. One open recipe trains a small LoRA adapter on Qwen3-ASR-0.6B, in hours on one GPU, to transcribe, detect end of turn, handle dictation and use context. On a deployment-matched benchmark it reaches 0.97 boundary recall at 0.39 s median latency with 0.3 false fires per speech-minute, and the result replicates on a fresh test set (arXiv 2609.04225). The paper's rule is that every streaming-decision label must be computable from input up to the decision point; offline corpora break this by encoding future audio. Appending one second of silence raised a supposedly broken model's end-of-turn recall from 0.10 to 1.00. For context biasing, an always-matching prefix caused 40% intrusion, and counterfactual examples that disagreed with the audio cut it to 0.8% while keeping most of a +28.9 percentage point entity-recall gain (arXiv 2609.04225).

The other route attaches a streaming ASR head to a duplex model (family 3d), so the user's words come from the same pass that decides whether the agent speaks (arXiv 2609.15759).

Production features

VibeVoice-ASR-Streaming returns speaker-attributed text in one autoregressive pass, with no separate diarization stage (arXiv 2609.02812). Hotword and entity handling are covered in Production features.

1. Full text, causal decoder (Qwen3-TTS). The tokenizer runs at 12.5 Hz and is causal with no look-ahead, so a packet of 4 tokens covers 320 ms. For the 0.6B model, time to first packet is 93 ms for the LM plus 4 ms of decode, 97 ms in total; the 1.7B model takes 101 ms (Table 2; arXiv 2601.15621). The Sesame CSM README documents a single generate call that returns a complete clip and no streaming mode (github.com/SesameAILabs/csm).

2. Incremental text, interleaved. CosyVoice 2 streaming mixes text and speech tokens at N = 5 to M = 15 (arXiv 2412.10117). SyncSpeech starts speaking after 2 text tokens, where CosyVoice 2 needs 5; its first packet takes 0.06 s against 0.22 s for CosyVoice 2 on LibriSpeech test-clean (arXiv 2502.11094). SpeakStream emits each word's speech after the word, using forced-aligned boundaries, at about 30 ms TTS latency (arXiv 2505.19206). This family fits a TTS that consumes another LLM's streamed output.

3. AR LM plus chunk-wise causal flow. CosyVoice 2 trains one flow-matching model under four masks: full-causal, chunk-M, chunk-2M and non-causal. Each mask sets how much future context a chunk sees (arXiv 2412.10117). The paper gives latency formulas, not a measured first packet. Kimi-Audio cuts audio into chunks of about 1 s and appends the next n semantic tokens (for example 4) from the following chunk before decoding. This is training-free and delays the first chunk by n tokens (arXiv 2504.18425, section 2.4).

4. Multi-token prediction (VITA-Audio). The MCTP module predicts about 10 audio tokens per forward pass. The audio token delay is 0, against 1 for Moshi and 13 for GLM-4-Voice (Table 1). The streaming first chunk drops from 236 ms to 53 ms (Table 9; arXiv 2505.03739).

5. Fixed ratio in dialogue models. GLM-4-Voice interleaves 13 text : 26 speech tokens in 0.8 s blocks (arXiv 2412.02612). MinMo uses 5 semantic : 15 speech tokens, with about 600 ms end to end (arXiv 2501.06282). OmniFlatten uses 2 : 10 and reports 193 ms against Moshi at 553 ms (arXiv 2410.17799). LLaMA-Omni 2 reads R = 3 and writes W = 10 tokens; its first speech chunk takes about 583 ms on 7B, the sum of LLM, TTS, flow and vocoder time (arXiv 2505.02625).

Inference A sixth family, non-autoregressive or flow models run chunk by chunk, is not covered because I found no primary streaming paper for F5 or ZipVoice.

How to choose

Design rules (labelled opinion, derived from the sources above)
  • Text is complete before speech starts: use a full-text AR codec LM with a causal decoder (Qwen3-TTS reaches 97 ms).
  • Text arrives token by token from another LLM: use an interleaved ratio model (CosyVoice 2, SyncSpeech).
  • ASR needs partials at a fixed, acceptable delay: use a DSM-style delayed stream, ideally with the delay as a runtime input (Voxtral Realtime). SpeechLLM-XL gives the WER-versus-chunk curve if you prefer chunks.
  • Speed matters more than a language model at decode time: a CTC-only encoder (TurboCTC) is the baseline; multi-token prediction narrows the gap for LLM decoders.
  • Turn-taking matters: measure emission delay per word and consider end-of-turn detection inside the recogniser, with labels computable from past audio only.
  • You already run Whisper: use Simul-Whisper for no retraining, or LocalAgreement for simplicity at the cost of more WER at the same latency.

Inference This maps onto the unified speech LLM design. The listen head is a delayed-stream or chunked ASR. The speak head is incremental-text interleaved TTS, consuming the tokens that the downstream reasoning LLM streams out.

In the interview, say:

"Streaming ASR comes in a few families: chunks with an end-of-chunk token, a per-frame wait token, a delayed text stream (now with the delay as an input), a learned read/write policy, a CTC-only encoder, or a read/write wrapper on top of Whisper. Streaming TTS depends on whether text arrives complete or token by token, so I would use interleaved text and speech for a speak head fed by another LLM. For listening I would take a delayed stream or chunked ASR, because chunk length or delay is the one dial that trades WER for latency, and I would measure latency per word since the setting is not what users feel."

Trap

The sources contradict themselves in places. DSM-TTS appears with 1.28 s in one place and 1.6 s in another. Simul-Whisper's abstract and its Table 1 disagree, and MinMo's abstract (about 100 ms speech to text) differs from section 4.5 (95 and 150 ms). Numbers from different hardware and test sets are not comparable.
