Audio-visual problems, grounded in Synthesia

Synthesia's Express-2 stack (September 2025) has four parts: Express-Voice for speech, Express-Animate for motion, Express-Eval for motion scoring, and Express-Render for frames Confirmed (Express-2 post, 4 September 2025). Each stage can introduce its own failures, and later stages cannot always repair them. The figure shows where each problem (B1 to B11 and A-codes from the previous section) enters; the placement is my mapping Inference.

B1 Lip-sync, and why Sync-C misleads

Hard cases plausibly include bilabial closures (p, b, m), fast speech and non-English audio Inference. The standard metrics are Sync-C and Sync-D, also written LSE-C and LSE-D, which are based on SyncNet (arXiv 2403.06421). SyncNet is a pretrained network that embeds mouth video and audio; Sync-D is the average Euclidean distance between the embeddings (lower is better) and Sync-C is the lip-audio synchronisation confidence (higher is better) (arXiv 2405.04327). Joint-generation papers add their own scores: JavisBench proposes a synchrony metric on 10,140 captioned sounding videos (arXiv 2503.23377). Evidence that the SyncNet scores mislead:

  • THEval's authors say existing metrics show limited correlation with human evaluation and unreasonable values, such as ground truth being widely outperformed (arXiv 2511.04520).
  • In a psychophysical comparison of metrics on talking-head videos, only LSE-O showed moderate effectiveness (arXiv 2403.06421).

Both are Reported results from papers. One proposed replacement uses an audio-visual speech representation expert (AV-HuBERT) for a lip-sync loss and three new evaluation metrics (arXiv 2405.04327). Sync-C values from different papers use different protocols, so I treat cross-paper tables as unreliable Inference. See metrics and data.

B2, B3 Identity drift, flicker and colour drift

Express-Render is described as keeping identity consistent over long sequences, with no metric attached Confirmed (Express-2 post). A related evaluation framework studies lip leakage, where the identity reference image influences the lips instead of the driving audio (arXiv 2511.08613) Reported. Open avatar work offers fixes for long sequences: sparse-frame conditioning to preserve identity (InfiniteTalk, arXiv 2508.14033) and a multi-step retrospective self-correction mechanism against accumulated error (SoulX-FlashTalk, arXiv 2512.23379) Reported. Flicker around teeth and mouth interior is a plausible weak spot Inference. The mechanisms are explained in streaming and real-time.

B4, B5, B6 Gesture and expressiveness

Express-2 avatars combine facial expressions and lip sync with natural hand and body gestures, and the post publishes no failure rates Confirmed. Express-Animate generates gestures from audio alone, which decouples motion from appearance Confirmed. Audio-only conditioning plausibly yields rhythm without intent Inference. OmniHuman-1.5 makes the same argument: earlier avatar models synchronise with low-level cues such as audio rhythm and lack semantic understanding of emotion and intent, so it adds MLLM-written semantic guidance (arXiv 2508.19209) Reported.

Express-2 also exposes performance intensity and temporal diversity controls Confirmed. To measure voice-face mismatch, compare emotion2vec on the audio with a facial-expression classifier on the video Inference.

B7 Dubbing

Synthesia's dubbing translates uploaded video into 30+ languages with frame-accurate lip-sync Confirmed (Synthesia 3.0 post). The post gives no lip-sync or voice-retention metrics.

The hard constraint is duration: translated speech is often longer than the source line, so the system must shorten the translation, speed the speech, or retime the video Inference. Measure the fraction of segments outside a timing budget, then lip-sync quality after retiming.

Two newer routes avoid the retiming chain. Synthesia's Dubbing 2.0 (15 July 2026) describes a translation engine that "respects the original timing and length, so far fewer segments need extension or manual repair" and a lip-sync model upgraded to stay "frame-accurate" through cuts and multi-speaker scenes (post). The post gives no timing or sync metrics Confirmed. The research route is joint generation: JUST-DUB-IT (arXiv 2601.22143) adapts an audio-video diffusion model with a lightweight LoRA to generate the translated audio and synchronised facial motion together, and reports better visual fidelity, lip sync and robustness than existing dubbing pipelines Reported. Inference Joint dubbing removes the duration-fitting step but moves voice-identity control into the video model; compare voice similarity before adopting it.

B8, B9 Streaming, resolution and compression

The Express-2 post gives no latency numbers. It says generation times should stay similar after the two-step distillation, and describes Express-Render-Turbo as coming later Confirmed. The quality versus latency trade-off is unpublished Inference.

Causal streaming students show their own failures. LiveTalk reports that Self Forcing, the leading on-policy distillation approach, shows flickering, black frames and quality degradation under multimodal conditioning, and proposes an improved distillation recipe (arXiv 2512.23576) Reported. Error accumulation plausibly grows with rollout length, and audio and video clocks can drift apart Inference. Output is 1080p at 30 fps with no stated duration limit Confirmed. Because encoder settings can change metric values, pin the encode settings before scoring Inference.

B12 Listening

Synthesia's "three levels" post says every interactive avatar currently on the market is Level 1, driven only by its own audio (post). Confirmed The failure is therefore absence: no nod, no reaction to a laugh or frown. VideoFDB (arXiv 2605.30256) is the first benchmark for full-duplex audio-visual-to-audio-visual agents, with 237 dyadic call clips across 11 nonverbal dynamics. Its authors report that models often collapse into generic captioning, largely ignore the visual stream, and can answer explicit visual questions without continuously combining audio and video; cascaded speech-to-avatar systems cannot produce full-duplex nonverbal cues Reported. On the generation side, FacePlex (arXiv 2606.30145) reports 122 ms end-to-end avatar interaction by predicting a short speech continuation so facial motion is ready before the audio plays, and Avatar Forcing (arXiv 2601.00664) reports about 500 ms with motion preferred in over 80% of comparisons Reported. See the real-time avatar section.

B11 Safety

Synthesia requires a live consent video that must show the same person as the photo, and does not accept uploaded consent videos Confirmed (personal avatars docs). It states that it strictly prohibits cloning voices without explicit consent (voice cloning page) and moderates content at the point of creation Confirmed. Its AI governance page lists a Content Integrity Policy, EU AI Act Article 50 labelling and Content Authenticity Initiative engagement. The page does not mention C2PA or watermarking.
