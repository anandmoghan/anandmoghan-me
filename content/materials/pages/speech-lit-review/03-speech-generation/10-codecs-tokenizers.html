Codecs and speech tokenizers

The tokenizer fixes the sequence length the LM sees and the ceiling on audio quality. Seven families matter; scaled Transformer-only codecs, dynamic frame rate and speaker-invariant tokens are 2025 to 2026 additions.

Family Examples Key facts
RVQ with semantic distillation Mimi (arXiv 2410.00037), SpeechTokenizer (arXiv 2308.16692) Mimi: causal, 12.5 Hz, 8 codebooks, 1.1 kbps, first codebook distilled from WavLM. SpeechTokenizer: RVQ whose first layers carry semantic information distilled from a HuBERT teacher, with acoustic detail in later layers.
Single-codebook, low rate WavTokenizer (arXiv 2408.16532), X-Codec2 (arXiv 2502.04128), BiCodec (arXiv 2503.01710), ReLMCodec (arXiv 2608.08286), BiMTokenizer (arXiv 2609.00562), NanoCodec (arXiv 2508.05835) WavTokenizer: 40 or 75 tokens/s. X-Codec2: single-layer FSQ codec at a 50 Hz latent rate, with Wav2Vec2-BERT semantic features (arXiv 2502.04128, arXiv 2601.20185). BiCodec: semantic tokens plus fixed-length global speaker tokens. ReLMCodec: single codebook at 650 and 800 bps; a training-only WavLM-Large teacher and limits on drift before quantization make the tokens easier for an LM to predict. BiMTokenizer: single-tower codec at about 1.1 kbps with Residual Spherical Leech Quantization; the authors report the lowest WER among low-bitrate baselines with under half the parameters of dual-tower models. NanoCodec: NVIDIA codec at 12.5 frames per second, built from ablations on frame rate, bitrate and causality.
FSQ tokens CosyVoice 2 (arXiv 2412.10117) Replaces VQ in the speech tokenizer with finite scalar quantization to improve codebook utilisation.
Transformer-only, scaled MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer (arXiv 2602.10934) CAT, a causal Transformer codec trained end to end with no pretrained encoder and no semantic distillation: 1.6B parameters, 3M hours of audio. The authors report gains that grow predictably with scale across speech, sound and music, and competitive ASR without auxiliary encoders. The abstract gives no bitrate or frame rate.
Dynamic frame rate FlexiCodec (arXiv 2510.00981), LACE (arXiv 2609.17509), Q-SPT (arXiv 2610.01492), ZipCodec (arXiv 2609.11642) FlexiCodec merges frames with similar semantic content, so the frame rate is controllable from 3 Hz to 12.5 Hz at inference; tested at 6.25, 8.3 and 12.5 Hz average rates. LACE compresses independently at each quantizer layer, so layers need not share segment boundaries. Q-SPT uses two learnable query compressors (semantic, supervised by an autoregressive text loss; acoustic) at a fixed low rate instead of rule-based pooling; the authors report the best reconstruction at equal frame rate. ZipCodec is a fixed ultra-low-rate streaming codec: 6.25 Hz, 0.80 kbps, 160 ms theoretical latency, large-scale WavLM distillation and a scalar spherical quantizer; 842M parameters, real time on a consumer CPU.
Speaker-invariant tokens PINT (arXiv 2607.19033) Fine-tunes a self-supervised encoder with alignment losses across parallel utterances and augmentations, so tokens keep only content shared across speakers. Speaker-probe accuracy falls from 93.1% to 1.2%, ABX error is 42% lower and LM perplexity 27 to 30% lower; tokens keep frame-level timing, so they can serve as semantic targets for codecs.
Continuous latents VibeVoice (arXiv 2508.19205), MELLE (arXiv 2407.08551) VibeVoice tokenizer: 7.5 Hz frame rate; the authors report 80x better compression than EnCodec at comparable performance. MELLE argues that vector quantization sacrifices fidelity compared to continuous representations.

Qwen3-TTS ships two tokenizers: 25 Hz single-codebook and 12 Hz multi-codebook (arXiv 2601.15621).

What "first codebook distilled from WavLM" means

Mimi trains its first quantizer to carry phonetic content, not just sound (arXiv 2410.00037). The teacher is WavLM, a self-supervised speech model producing 1024-dim embeddings at 50 Hz. During training, the first quantizer's output passes through a linear projection to 1024 dims, and a cosine-distance loss pulls it toward WavLM's embedding for the same moment. WavLM's features are average-pooled (stride 4, kernel 8) from 50 Hz to Mimi's 12.5 Hz.

Mimi is fully causal, while WavLM sees the whole utterance. The teacher is used only in training, so its look-ahead never reaches inference. Distilling into level 1 of a normal RVQ made semantic and acoustic goals conflict, because later levels encode level 1's residual. Mimi therefore runs one plain VQ for semantics in parallel with a 7-level RVQ for acoustics and sums them; the authors report distillation improves phonetic discriminability (ABX) and the split gives a better semantic-acoustic trade-off.

Distillation is not the only route to LM-friendly tokens. MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer (arXiv 2602.10934) argues for learning the tokenizer end to end with a uniform architecture instead of pretrained encoders or semantic distillation, and reports that it improves predictably with scale. ReLMCodec (arXiv 2608.08286) keeps a WavLM-Large teacher in training only. JHCodec (arXiv 2603.05887) asks the decoder output to reconstruct distilled self-supervised features, which speeds convergence (competitive after 300k steps on one H200) and gives the best WER and CER on LibriSpeech test-clean among the codecs compared, with zero lookahead. PINT (arXiv 2607.19033) removes speaker information from a purely semantic path. Training-side detail is in Training for predictable tokens.

Evidence on low frame rates

A controlled frame-rate ablation reproduces a quality cliff at 6.25 Hz. Its authors trace the cliff to the training configuration (fixed clip duration gives too few tokens at low rates), not to a fundamental barrier. After the fix, WER degrades smoothly with phonemic load down to 3.1 Hz and 1.6 Hz (arXiv 2606.16969). Systems built at those rates agree: ZipCodec runs at 6.25 Hz and 0.80 kbps and reports better reconstruction and downstream results than existing streaming codecs at comparable bitrates (arXiv 2609.11642), and FlexiCodec reports strong semantic preservation at a 6.25 Hz average (arXiv 2510.00981). So 6.25 Hz is no longer a floor, though each of these systems relies on distillation, text or merging to get there. Another paper pools X-Codec-2.0 from 50 Hz to a 25 Hz latent rate and reports a 0.29 UTMOSv2-based MOS gain (arXiv 2601.20185).

Say this in the interview:

"The trade is rate against fidelity. Low or dynamic rate and tokens trained to be predictable make the LM's job easy; continuous latents or a flow decoder win back what quantization loses."
