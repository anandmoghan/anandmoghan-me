How codecs are trained: VQ-VAE, RVQ and continuous latents

An RVQ codec is a VQ-VAE-family autoencoder: encoder, discrete bottleneck, decoder, trained end to end. It is not a VAE. The VQ-VAE paper does view its model as a VAE with a deterministic posterior and a uniform prior, which makes the KL term a constant (\( \log K \)) that is dropped from training (arXiv 1711.00937). So no KL loss and no sampling appear in an RVQ codec. Continuous-latent tokenizers are the VAE counterpart: they keep a Gaussian bottleneck and a KL term instead of a codebook. What these tokenizers produce is described in Codecs and speech tokenizers.

VQ-VAE: the base recipe

The encoder output \( z_e(x) \) is replaced by its nearest codebook vector \( e_k \), with \( k = \arg\min_j \lVert z_e(x) - e_j \rVert_2 \), and the decoder reads \( z_q(x) = e_k \) (arXiv 1711.00937). The argmin has no gradient, so the decoder's gradient at \( z_q \) is copied unchanged to \( z_e \) (a straight-through estimator). Reconstruction therefore never updates the codebook, so two extra terms are added. The codebook loss pulls code vectors toward encoder outputs, and the commitment loss keeps encoder outputs from drifting away from their codes.

\[ \mathcal{L} = \log p\big(x \mid z_q(x)\big) + \lVert \operatorname{sg}[z_e(x)] - e \rVert_2^2 + \beta \lVert z_e(x) - \operatorname{sg}[e] \rVert_2^2 \]

Source: VQ-VAE, Eq. 3 (arXiv 1711.00937). Here \( \operatorname{sg} \) is the stop-gradient operator, identity in the forward pass and zero partial derivatives in the backward pass. The decoder trains on the first term only, the encoder on the first and third, and the codebook on the second. The paper uses \( \beta = 0.25 \) and reports little change for \( \beta \) from 0.1 to 2.0. Its appendix describes an EMA update for the codebook instead of the second term (decay \( \gamma = 0.99 \)), but states the main experiments did not use it (arXiv 1711.00937). Inference The first term is a log-likelihood to maximise; in a codec it becomes a reconstruction error to minimise.

RVQ: SoundStream, EnCodec, DAC

Residual vector quantization chains \( N_q \) quantizers, each coding what the previous ones missed (SoundStream, Algorithm 1; arXiv 2107.03312).

\[ \hat y \leftarrow 0,\quad r_0 \leftarrow y;\qquad \text{for } i = 1,\dots,N_q:\;\; \hat y \leftarrow \hat y + Q_i(r_{i-1}),\;\; r_i \leftarrow r_{i-1} - Q_i(r_{i-1}) \]

SoundStream (arXiv 2107.03312) trains this end to end with the following recipe.

  • Quantizer dropout: for each example, sample \( n_q \) uniformly in \( [1, N_q] \) and use only the first \( n_q \) quantizers. One model then serves many bitrates.
  • Codebooks use EMA updates (following VQ-VAE-2) with k-means initialisation on the first batch. A code with no assigned frames for several batches is replaced by a random frame from the batch. The EMA decay is 0.99, and codes whose EMA falls below 2 are replaced.
  • Generator loss: adversarial, feature matching and multi-scale spectral reconstruction, with \( \lambda_{adv} = 1 \), \( \lambda_{feat} = 100 \), \( \lambda_{rec} = 1 \). Its generator loss (Eq. 6) lists no commitment term.
  • Streamable by design: all convolutions are causal, and the encoder strides (2, 4, 5, 8) give one embedding per 320 samples. With \( N_q = 8 \), each codebook has 1024 entries.

EnCodec keeps the recipe and changes the training signal (arXiv 2210.13438).

  • It adds a commitment loss \( \sum_c \lVert z_c - q_c(z_c) \rVert_2^2 \) over the residual steps, with gradient only into the quantizer input.
  • It uses a multi-scale STFT discriminator with five window lengths (2048 to 128).
  • A loss balancer rescales each loss's gradient with respect to the output \( \hat x \). With weights \( \lambda_i \), reference norm \( R \) and the EMA \( \langle \lVert g_i \rVert_2 \rangle_\beta \) of each gradient norm, it backpropagates the following. The commitment loss is excluded.
\[ \tilde g_i = R \, \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_j \lambda_j} \cdot \frac{g_i}{\langle \lVert g_i \rVert_2 \rangle_\beta}, \qquad g_i = \frac{\partial \ell_i}{\partial \hat x} \]

Source: EnCodec, Eq. 5 (arXiv 2210.13438), with \( R = 1 \) and \( \beta = 0.999 \). At 24 kHz EnCodec produces 75 latent steps per second. It uses up to 32 codebooks of 1024 entries, and trains for 1.5, 3, 6, 12 and 24 kbps by picking a multiple of 4 codebooks per batch (arXiv 2210.13438).

DAC (arXiv 2306.06546) targets codebook collapse, where many codes go unused. It does code lookup in a low-dimensional space (8 or 32 dimensions) while the code embedding stays 1024-dimensional. It L2-normalises encoder outputs and codes, so distance becomes cosine similarity. With this it trains on plain VQ-VAE codebook and commitment losses (weights 1.0 and 0.25), without k-means initialisation or random restarts. The authors found that applying quantizer dropout to every example hurts full-bandwidth quality, and apply it with probability \( p = 0.5 \) instead. Other weights are 15.0 for the multi-scale mel loss, 2.0 for feature matching and 1.0 for adversarial, with no loss balancer. Reported setup: 44.1 kHz audio at 8 kbps, 86 Hz frame rate, 9 codebooks (arXiv 2306.06546).

FSQ: a codebook-free alternative

Finite scalar quantization bounds each of \( d \) channels and rounds it to one of \( L \) integer levels (arXiv 2309.15505).

\[ \hat z = \operatorname{round}\!\big(\lfloor L/2 \rfloor \tanh(z)\big), \qquad |\mathcal{C}| = \prod_{i=1}^{d} L_i \]

Source: FSQ, Sec. 3 (arXiv 2309.15505). The implicit codebook is the product of the per-channel level sets. Gradients pass through the rounding with a straight-through estimator, implemented as \( x + \operatorname{sg}(\operatorname{round}(x) - x) \). The reconstruction loss alone pushes information across bins, so there is no codebook loss, no commitment loss and no EMA. The authors report codebook usage near 100% for most models, without auxiliary losses (arXiv 2309.15505). CosyVoice 2 swaps VQ for FSQ in its speech tokenizer, which works at 25 Hz (arXiv 2412.10117). That tokenizer is an ASR encoder with FSQ inserted (SenseVoice-Large), trained to predict text, so it is supervised rather than a reconstruction autoencoder. The authors report that the FSQ tokenizer fully uses its codebook (arXiv 2412.10117).

Mimi: adversarial-only training

Mimi uses 8 quantizers with 2048 entries at 12.5 Hz, 1.1 kbps, with quantizer dropout for bitrate scalability (arXiv 2410.00037). It applies quantization to only 50% of training sequences and passes unquantized embeddings to the decoder otherwise. It also drops the reconstruction loss and keeps only the feature-matching and discriminator losses. The authors report that this lowers objective scores (VisQOL 1.84) but sounds better: MUSHRA 81.0 against 58.8 for the EnCodec-style mix of reconstruction and adversarial losses (arXiv 2410.00037). The semantic distillation loss and the split RVQ are covered in What "first codebook distilled from WavLM" means.

Training for predictable tokens

Reconstruction-trained codecs are not optimised for the language model that reads their tokens. LLM-Codec (arXiv 2604.17852) argues the mismatch injects acoustically driven uncertainty into the discrete token space and raises LM perplexity. It keeps both codec and LLM architectures unchanged and adds two LM-facing objectives: future-token prediction with Medusa-style multi-step heads, and audio-text alignment through a memory-bank contrastive loss. A differentiable Gumbel bridge carries gradients from these objectives back to the codec encoder. Token LMs trained on it reach 61.6% on SALMon speech coherence, 12.1 points above AUV, and the authors report a 5.0% better speech Mel distance on Codec-SUPERB-tiny, so fidelity and predictability can improve together.

ReLMCodec (arXiv 2608.08286) reaches the same goal from the quantizer input. A controlled analysis finds that clearer phoneme structure before code assignment goes with tokens that are easier to predict, but that phoneme structure alone does not give high-fidelity reconstruction. The codec keeps the linguistic organisation of frozen self-supervised features at the quantizer input, limits the drift reconstruction causes (Pre-quantization Anchor-Preserving Adaptation), and uses a training-only WavLM-Large layer 24 teacher to reduce phoneme-level token fragmentation. At 650 and 800 bps it reports a better single-stream predictability-reconstruction frontier and gains in TTS intelligibility and speaker similarity.

JHCodec (arXiv 2603.05887) changes what the decoder must produce: a self-supervised representation reconstruction loss makes the decoder output reconstruct distilled self-supervised features. The authors report faster convergence (competitive after 300k steps on one H200) and the best WER and CER on LibriSpeech test-clean among the codecs evaluated, with zero lookahead. Inference These losses sit beside, not in place of, the reconstruction and adversarial terms above.

Continuous latents: the VAE side

A VAE learns an encoder \( q_\phi(z \mid x) \) and a decoder \( p_\theta(x \mid z) \) by maximising a variational lower bound (arXiv 1312.6114).

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta, \phi; x) = -D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q_\phi(z \mid x) \,\|\, p_\theta(z)\big) + \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z \mid x)}\big[\log p_\theta(x \mid z)\big] \]

Source: Kingma and Welling, Eq. 3 (arXiv 1312.6114). The reparameterisation trick draws \( z = \mu + \sigma \epsilon \) with \( \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \), which makes the sample differentiable. The KL term regularises the posterior toward the prior. Speech and audio tokenizers keep this shape and add the codec's adversarial and spectral losses.

Prior, posterior and the reparameterisation trick

  • Prior: in the paper's Gaussian example, the prior over latents is a centred isotropic Gaussian, \( p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I) \) (arXiv 1312.6114).
  • Posterior: the encoder outputs a mean and standard deviation per latent dimension, so \( q_\phi(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I) \) (arXiv 1312.6114).
  • With both Gaussian, the KL term has a closed form and needs no sampling (Eq. 10, arXiv 1312.6114):
\[ -D_{\mathrm{KL}} = \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{j} \left( 1 + \log \sigma_j^2 - \mu_j^2 - \sigma_j^2 \right) \]

Sampling is made differentiable by moving the randomness into a parameter-free noise variable (Section 2.4, arXiv 1312.6114):

\[ \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon \]

Now \( z \) is a deterministic function of \( \mu \) and \( \sigma \), with \( \partial z / \partial \mu = 1 \) and \( \partial z / \partial \sigma = \epsilon \) (derived), so reconstruction gradients flow back into the encoder. The KL pulls \( \mu \) toward 0 and \( \sigma \) toward 1, keeping the latent space smooth and bounded.

Inference At generation time nothing samples from \( \mathcal{N}(0, I) \): the LM's diffusion or flow head generates the next latent from context, and the decoder turns it into audio. The learned generator replaces the simple prior; the prior only shapes the latent space during tokenizer training.

Why go continuous: MELLE argues that vector quantization, designed for compression, sacrifices fidelity compared with continuous representations (arXiv 2407.08551). MELLE itself regresses mel frames with a variational latent sampling module, and leaves VAE latents for future work (arXiv 2407.08551). Evaluating tokenizers

Three questions are easy to confuse: does the codec rebuild the audio, can an LM predict its tokens, and are the tokens useful downstream.

Question Measures used in this review What they are
Reconstruction PESQ, ViSQOL, UTMOS, Mel distance, MUSHRA, WER of an ASR on decoded audio PESQ and ViSQOL compare decoded audio with the original; UTMOS predicts a mean opinion score; MUSHRA is a listening test with a hidden reference; WER measures intelligibility.
LM-predictability Perplexity of a decoder-only transformer trained on the tokens; codebook index stability; SALMon coherence Perplexity is the model's average surprise at the next token; SALMon tests whether a token LM prefers coherent speech.
Downstream Probe tasks, ABX, recognition and TTS quality (DASB, arXiv 2406.14294) ABX tests whether the representation separates phonemes; probes train a small classifier on frozen tokens.

AudioCodecBench (arXiv 2509.02349) evaluates along four axes: audio reconstruction, codebook ID stability, decoder-only-transformer perplexity and probe tasks. It also defines semantic and acoustic tokens, and the authors report that the measures correlate. A related study, "Language-Statistical Analysis of Neural Audio Codec Tokens Across Architectures, Corpora, and Noise Conditions" (arXiv 2608.31037), covers 13 codecs. LLM-Codec and ReLMCodec above evaluate with the predictability side (SALMon, perplexity, predictability-against-reconstruction frontier) as well as reconstruction.

Nominal against entropy-coded rate

The nominal bitrate \( f \times Q \times \log_2 K \) assumes every code is equally likely. "Benchmarking Neural Speech Compression from a Rate-Distortion Perspective" (arXiv 2606.11631) notes that many neural speech codecs use preset-rate discrete symbols, or apply entropy coding only after the symbols are generated, which separates representation learning from probability modelling. Its ECC codec pairs scalar quantization with a learned entropy model (hyperprior side information, channel-wise context modelling, latent residual prediction) and an "entropy skip" that omits highly predictable residual symbols. It reports average BD-rate reductions of 39.9% on ViSQOL and 76.3% on PESQ over two test sets. Inference A comparison at equal nominal bitrate between a fixed-rate codec and an entropy-coded one is therefore not like for like; compare at equal entropy-coded rate.

In the interview, say:

"An RVQ codec is a VQ-VAE-style autoencoder with a discrete bottleneck, trained with reconstruction, adversarial and commitment losses. A continuous tokenizer is the VAE version: a Gaussian latent with a KL term, which removes quantization loss but needs a diffusion or flow head. I evaluate on reconstruction, LM-predictability and downstream use together, because reconstruction-best tokens are not LM-best."
