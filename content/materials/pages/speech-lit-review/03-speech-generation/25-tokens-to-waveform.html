From tokens to waveform: decoder paths

Mel is one route to audio, not the only one. Four routes are in use, and they differ in what the LM predicts and what turns it into samples. Codecs and flow decoders are covered in the neighbouring sections; this one compares the endings.

Route Systems Reported detail
1. Codec tokens, codec decoder Moshi (arXiv 2410.00037), Qwen3-Omni (arXiv 2509.17765), MaskGCT (arXiv 2409.00750) Mimi is causal for both encoding and decoding, with an 80 ms frame (arXiv 2410.00037). Qwen3-Omni's Talker predicts the zeroth codebook, an MTP module predicts all residual codebooks, and a lightweight causal ConvNet (Code2Wav) makes the waveform (arXiv 2509.17765). MaskGCT's acoustic codec follows DAC but uses a Vocos decoder (arXiv 2409.00750).
2. Semantic tokens, flow matching, mel, vocoder CosyVoice 2 (arXiv 2412.10117), GLM-4-Voice (arXiv 2412.02612), Kimi-Audio (arXiv 2504.18425), Step-Audio 2 (arXiv 2507.16632) CosyVoice 2 samples a 50 Hz mel spectrogram, conditioned on speech tokens, reference speech and a speaker embedding; a pre-trained vocoder (unnamed in the paper) then produces audio (arXiv 2412.10117). GLM-4-Voice and Step-Audio 2 pair flow matching with HiFi-GAN (arXiv 2412.02612, arXiv 2507.16632). Kimi-Audio turns 12.5 Hz tokens into 50 Hz mel, then applies BigVGAN per chunk, with next-chunk tokens as look-ahead (arXiv 2504.18425).
3. Continuous latents, VAE decoder VibeVoice (arXiv 2508.19205), DiTTo-TTS (arXiv 2406.11427) VibeVoice's VAE tokenizer maps 24 kHz audio to 7.5 frames per second; a light diffusion head predicts each frame and the tokenizer decoder recovers the audio (arXiv 2508.19205). DiTTo-TTS diffuses in a Mel-VAE latent space; the decoder gives a mel spectrogram and BigVGAN finishes the job, so it ends like route 2 (arXiv 2406.11427).
4. Mel frames, vocoder MELLE (arXiv 2407.08551) Autoregressively generates continuous mel frames from text; an open-source HiFi-GAN vocoder reconstructs audio (arXiv 2407.08551).

Details on the LM side are in Codecs and speech tokenizers, Continuous autoregression and Diffusion and flow matching.

One-step and waveform-native endings

Newer work shortens or removes the decoder stage instead of choosing between the four routes.

  • One-step token-to-waveform: multi-step flow-matching decoders sound good but sample iteratively. The authors apply MeanFlow in a highly compressed latent space, modelling average velocity instead of instantaneous velocity, so one step is enough. They report an RTF up to 17x better than multi-step baselines with negligible quality degradation, and two refinements for latent mismatch: fine-tune the decoder with the MeanFlow generator frozen, or fine-tune end to end (arXiv 2606.18072). This collapses route 2's flow, mel and vocoder chain into one stage. Inference
  • Flow2GAN: flow-matching pretraining, then lightweight GAN fine-tuning for generators that run in 1, 2 or 4 steps. It predicts the endpoint instead of the velocity, which avoids difficulties in silent regions, scales the loss by spectral energy to weight quieter regions, and uses a multi-branch network over Fourier coefficients at several time-frequency resolutions. It decodes from mel or discrete audio tokens (arXiv 2512.23278; ICLR 2026 per the arXiv listing).
  • BareWave: flow-matching TTS that generates the waveform directly from text, with no intermediate acoustic representation and no pretrained component at test time. Training uses representation alignment, staged noise scheduling and velocity-aware perceptual alignment (arXiv 2606.09048).
  • VoxCPM2: a 2B continuous-latent TTS (over 2M h) whose AudioVAE encodes at 16 kHz and reconstructs at 48 kHz, so the decoder super-resolves implicitly; there is no separate super-resolution model (arXiv 2606.06928).

Codec decoder or vocoder

A codec decoder is trained together with its encoder and quantizer to rebuild the waveform from the codes. DAC is an example: a neural codec that turns audio into discrete tokens, with about 90x compression of 44.1 kHz audio at 8 kbps (arXiv 2306.06546). A vocoder is a separate model that maps an acoustic representation, usually a mel spectrogram, to a waveform. HiFi-GAN is a GAN vocoder that generates raw waveforms (arXiv 2010.05646). BigVGAN is a GAN vocoder built to generalise to unseen speakers and recording conditions without fine-tuning (arXiv 2206.04658).

Is the STFT reversible?

What you keep Reversible? How audio comes back
Complex STFT (magnitude and phase) Yes: inverse FFT per frame, then overlap-add, given a window and hop that overlap correctly Inverse STFT
Magnitude only No: phase is discarded, and many signals share one magnitude Phase retrieval: Griffin–Lim iterates between a phase estimate and a consistent STFT (Griffin and Lim, IEEE TASSP 1984); the Fast Griffin–Lim Algorithm accelerates it (Perraudin, Balazs and Søndergaard, IEEE WASPAA 2013)
Mel spectrogram No, and lossier: the mel filterbank also merges frequency bins Neural vocoders: HiFi-GAN (arXiv 2010.05646) and BigVGAN (arXiv 2206.04658) generate the waveform; Vocos directly generates Fourier spectral coefficients and reports an order-of-magnitude speedup over time-domain GAN vocoders at matching quality (arXiv 2306.00814)

Inference Mel features suit models but drop phase and fine frequency detail, so classical inversion is slow and artefact-prone; learned vocoders, or codec decoders that skip spectrograms entirely, replaced it.

What phase adds to the signal

Magnitude says how much energy sits at each frequency in a frame. Phase says where each component is in its cycle, so it fixes how the components line up in time.

  • Transients and waveform shape: a plosive or onset is sharp because many components peak at the same instant; keeping magnitude and scrambling phase smears that energy across the frame. Inference
  • Timing inside a frame: the group delay, \( \tau(\omega) = -\,d\phi(\omega)/d\omega \), locates energy in time more finely than the frame length (standard definition).
  • Fine pitch: the instantaneous frequency, the rate of phase change over time, places a harmonic more precisely than the FFT bin spacing (standard definition).
  • Frame consistency: overlapping STFT frames must agree for overlap-add to rebuild the signal; inconsistent phase is what phase retrieval has to repair (see the table above). Inference

The research record on how much phase matters:

  • Oppenheim and Lim argue that magnitude and phase play different roles, that many important features of a signal survive when only the phase is kept, and that a finite-length signal can be reconstructed from phase alone to within a scale factor; their examples include images and speech (Oppenheim and Lim, "The Importance of Phase in Signals", Proc. IEEE 69(5), 1981).
  • Paliwal and Alsteris challenge the traditional view that magnitude dominates for short (speech-length) windows: in their listening tests the short-time phase spectrum can contribute to intelligibility comparably to the magnitude spectrum when the window function and analysis-modification-synthesis parameters are chosen well (Paliwal and Alsteris, Eurospeech 2003; Speech Communication 2005).
  • The same line of work reports a mismatch for machines: with an MFCC front-end, recognition of phase-only stimuli at short windows was much worse than of magnitude-only stimuli (Alsteris and Paliwal, Interspeech 2004 and 2005).

Inference Models avoid predicting phase directly because it wraps every \( 2\pi \) and looks noisy frame to frame, so most systems model magnitude or mel and let a vocoder, Griffin–Lim or a codec decoder supply consistent phase; Vocos is the exception that generates the spectral coefficients itself (arXiv 2306.00814).

Why route 2 exists, and why route 1 streams

Inference Semantic tokens are cheap for an LM to predict but drop voice detail. A flow model restores it, conditioned on a speaker prompt (CosyVoice 2 uses reference speech and a speaker embedding, arXiv 2412.10117). The cost is extra stages and look-ahead, as in Kimi-Audio, where the look-ahead delays only the first chunk (arXiv 2504.18425). Chunk-wise streaming of this kind is covered in Streaming and low latency.

Route 1 has one stage after the LM, and that stage can be causal. Qwen3-Omni can output the waveform immediately after the Talker generates each token (arXiv 2509.17765). Inference The price is on the LM side: it must predict every acoustic codebook of each frame. Moshi uses a depth transformer for that and Qwen3-Omni an MTP module, as described in Generation paradigms.

Why not predict samples directly

Inference A waveform at 24 kHz has 24,000 samples per second. Mimi emits 12.5 frames per second (arXiv 2410.00037), so one frame stands for 1,920 samples. Predicting samples one by one would make the sequence 1,920 times longer than a frame-rate LM sees. That is why most systems predict tokens or latents and leave sample-level detail to a decoder. BareWave is the exception: it runs flow matching on the waveform itself (see above), so the cost shifts from sequence length to network size and training difficulty. Inference

In the interview, say:

"Mel is one route among four. Streaming duplex systems like Moshi and Qwen3-Omni skip it: the LM predicts codec tokens and a causal codec decoder makes samples frame by frame. Mel plus a vocoder buys voice quality through a flow stage, at the cost of more stages and look-ahead."
