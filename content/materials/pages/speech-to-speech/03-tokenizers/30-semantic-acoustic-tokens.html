Semantic against acoustic tokens

A codec trained only to rebuild audio spends its bits on how the sound was made: the speaker, the room, the noise. A language model wants what was said. Acoustic tokens know how it sounded; semantic tokens know what it meant.

Compare a photograph with a caption. The photograph rebuilds the scene and is hard to reason about. The caption is easy to reason about and cannot rebuild the scene. Semantic distillation tries to put a bit of the caption inside the photograph.

How it works

Mimi trains its first quantizer to carry phonetic content, not just sound. The teacher is WavLM, a self-supervised speech model (trained on unlabelled audio, with no transcripts) that produces 1024-dimensional embeddings at 50 Hz. During training, the quantizer's output goes through a linear projection to 1024 dimensions, and a cosine-distance loss (small when two vectors point the same way) pulls it toward WavLM's embedding for the same moment. WavLM's features are average-pooled from 50 Hz to Mimi's 12.5 Hz to match. The teacher sees the whole utterance while Mimi is fully causal, which is fine: the teacher is used only in training, so its look-ahead never reaches inference.

Why not simply distil into level 1 of a normal RVQ? Because the two goals pull the same quantizer in opposite directions. A normal RVQ expects level 1 to be the best coarse copy of the audio, and every later level codes what level 1 missed. Suppose level 1 is forced to keep only phonetic content, so the word "hello" gets nearly the same code from a deep voice in a quiet room and a high voice in an echoing hall. The speaker's voice and the room then cannot go in level 1, so they all land in the residual. Level 1 is no longer a good coarse copy of the sound, and the later levels must carry a larger and messier residual. Mimi therefore runs one plain VQ for semantics in parallel with a 7-level RVQ for acoustics, and sums them, so the acoustic RVQ can still start from the full signal. The authors report better phonetic discriminability and a better semantic-acoustic trade-off from the split.

A second line of work asks for tokens that are easy to predict, not only easy to rebuild. Acoustic noise in the tokens makes the next token harder to guess and raises the LM's perplexity (its average surprise at the next token). ReLMCodec keeps the linguistic structure of frozen self-supervised features before quantizing and limits how far training can pull the features away from it; at 650 and 800 bps it reports a better predictability-against-reconstruction frontier and better TTS intelligibility and speaker similarity (how closely the output voice matches the reference voice; how it is scored). LLM-Codec trains the codec with language-model losses directly: predicting several future tokens from each token, and an audio-text contrastive loss. Both suggest a codec for an LM is judged by what the LM can do with its tokens (see Judging a tokenizer).

Distillation is common but not required. MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer is a Transformer-only causal codec (1.6B parameters, trained on 3 million hours of audio) learned end to end with no pretrained encoder and no semantic distillation. Its authors report that it beats earlier codecs across speech, sound and music, improves predictably with scale, and supports competitive speech recognition without auxiliary encoders. Whether this holds at equal bitrate against distilled codecs remains open. Inference

Other routes reach similar ends. Instead of a self-supervised teacher, GLM-4-Voice puts a single-codebook VQ on an ASR encoder at 12.5 Hz, and CosyVoice 2's tokenizer is an ASR encoder with FSQ inserted, trained to predict text. BiCodec pairs semantic tokens with fixed-length global speaker tokens. PINT goes furthest: it fine-tunes a self-supervised encoder so that the same words spoken by different speakers or under different conditions get the same tokens, which cut speaker-probe accuracy from 93.1% to 1.2% and LM perplexity by 27 to 30%. Its tokens keep frame-level timing, unlike a text transcript.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Semantic distillation Pulls a quantizer toward a teacher's features Easier LM modelling and better phonetic content, against conflict with reconstruction quality.
Split against plain RVQ Whether semantics sits in the first RVQ level or in a parallel branch Moshi's authors report the listening score MUSHRA recovering from 57.8 to 64.0 with the split, at a small cost in ABX (does the representation keep different phonemes apart; lower error is better). Both are explained in Judging a tokenizer.
Predictability losses Future-token prediction and audio-text contrastive loss (LLM-Codec); a limit on feature drift before quantization (ReLMCodec) Easier for the LM to predict, if tuned so reconstruction does not fall.
Teacher choice WavLM (Mimi), an ASR encoder (GLM-4-Voice, CosyVoice 2), or no teacher (MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer) Self-supervised teachers need no text; ASR teachers are supervised by text and give content-focused tokens, with a separate decoder to rebuild sound. No teacher gives a simpler pipeline that scales with data, against the content bias a teacher gives.
Speaker invariance or separate speaker tokens PINT trains tokens to be the same across speakers; BiCodec holds global speaker information outside the per-frame tokens Cleaner content tokens and lower LM perplexity, with the speaker supplied separately.

What goes wrong

  • Semantic and acoustic goals conflict. Distilling into level 1 of a normal RVQ pulls it away from reconstruction, and the later levels then encode its residual, speaker and room included. The split design exists to fix this.
  • Best reconstruction is not best downstream. The DASB benchmark (a suite that tests discrete audio tokens on recognition, understanding and generation tasks) reports that discrete audio representations are less robust than continuous ones, and that semantic tokens generally outperform acoustic tokens.
  • Content without character. Inference Pure semantic tokens drop speaker and prosody detail, so some other path (acoustic codes, speaker tokens, a flow decoder) must restore it.

In the interview, say:

Acoustic tokens rebuild the sound, semantic tokens carry the content, and the LM wants the content. Mimi distils a self-supervised teacher into a separate plain VQ and keeps a 7-level RVQ for acoustics, because distilling into level 1 of a normal RVQ conflicts with reconstruction. Newer work asks for tokens the LM can predict, and shows distillation is optional once a codec is scaled up.
