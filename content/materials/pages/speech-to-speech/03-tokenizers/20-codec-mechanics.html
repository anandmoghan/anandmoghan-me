How a codec works

A codec is an autoencoder with a deliberately narrow, discrete middle. The encoder compresses audio into one vector per frame. A quantizer snaps each vector to the nearest entry of a learned codebook, like rounding to the nearest note. The decoder rebuilds audio from the snapped values, and the token is the index of the entry.

How it works

Vector quantization (VQ). The encoder output \( z_e \) is replaced by its nearest codebook vector \( e_k \), and the code is the index \( k \).

\[ k = \arg\min_j \lVert z_e - e_j \rVert_2 \]

One codebook of \( K = 2048 \) entries gives 11 bits per frame, or 137.5 bits per second at 12.5 frames per second. That is too little for high-quality audio, so Mimi stacks eight such codebooks (the bitrate arithmetic is in Why tokenize).

Residual vector quantization (RVQ). Chain \( N_q \) quantizers, each coding what the previous ones missed. The first code is coarse, and later codes add detail.

\[ \hat y \leftarrow 0,\quad r_0 \leftarrow y;\qquad \text{for } i = 1,\dots,N_q:\;\; \hat y \leftarrow \hat y + Q_i(r_{i-1}),\;\; r_i \leftarrow r_{i-1} - Q_i(r_{i-1}) \]

Here \( y \) is the encoder's vector for one frame (the \( z \) in the figure) and \( \hat y \) is the running reconstruction of it. Each \( Q_i \) is its own VQ with its own codebook: it returns the nearest entry and sends that entry's index as the \( i \)-th code. The residual \( r_i \) is what is still unexplained after \( i \) stages, and the decoder sums what every quantizer chose. Keeping fewer quantizers lowers the bitrate. A toy two-stage example with 2-D vectors (made-up numbers, for intuition only):

Stage Input Nearest codebook entry Running sum \( \hat y \) Left over
Start \( y = (5.0,\, 3.0) \) \( (0,\, 0) \) \( r_0 = (5.0,\, 3.0) \)
\( Q_1 \) \( r_0 \) \( (4,\, 4) \) \( (4.0,\, 4.0) \) \( r_1 = (1.0,\, -1.0) \), length 1.41
\( Q_2 \) \( r_1 \) \( (1.5,\, -1.5) \) \( (5.5,\, 2.5) \) \( r_2 = (-0.5,\, 0.5) \), length 0.71

Each stage shrinks the error (1.41, then 0.71), and the decoder only needs the two indices. Drop stage 2 and the decoder sees \( (4, 4) \): cheaper, rougher.

Finite scalar quantization (FSQ). Skip the learned codebook. Bound each of \( d \) channels and round it to one of \( L \) integer levels.

\[ \hat z = \operatorname{round}\big(\lfloor L/2 \rfloor \tanh(z)\big), \qquad |\mathcal{C}| = \prod_{i=1}^{d} L_i \]

The \( \tanh \) squashes any real number into \( (-1, 1) \), so multiplying by \( \lfloor L/2 \rfloor \) and rounding always lands on one of the \( L \) allowed integers. For \( L = 5 \) the allowed values are \( -2, -1, 0, 1, 2 \): a channel value of \( z = 0.3 \) becomes \( \tanh(0.3) \approx 0.29 \), then \( 2 \times 0.29 \approx 0.58 \), which rounds to 1. The implicit codebook is the product of the per-channel level counts. Four channels with 8, 5, 5 and 5 levels give \( 8 \times 5 \times 5 \times 5 = 1000 \) possible codes, with no table to store or to collapse. CosyVoice 2 uses FSQ in its speech tokenizer at 25 Hz, with a 6,561-entry implicit codebook that it reports as fully used.

How it is trained

An RVQ codec is trained end to end like a VQ-VAE. It is not a full VAE: it has no sampled latent and no penalty toward a simple distribution, which the last section of this chapter adds.

The training signal has two obstacles. First, the argmin has no gradient, so the straight-through trick copies the decoder's gradient at the quantized vector unchanged to the encoder output. Second, reconstruction never updates the codebook, because the argmin only picks a vector. So two terms are added: one pulls code vectors toward encoder outputs, the other keeps encoder outputs from drifting away from their codes.

\[ \mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \underbrace{\lVert \operatorname{sg}[z_e(x)] - e \rVert_2^2}_{\text{codebook}} + \underbrace{\beta \lVert z_e(x) - \operatorname{sg}[e] \rVert_2^2}_{\text{commitment}} \]

The original paper writes the first term as a log-likelihood to maximise; this is the same objective as a quantity to minimise (derived).

The notation

Symbol Meaning
\( x \), \( \hat{x} \) The input audio and the decoder's reconstruction of it.
\( z_e(x) \) The encoder's continuous output vector for one frame ("e" for encoder).
\( e \) The codebook vector closest to \( z_e(x) \), chosen by the argmin.
\( z_q(x) \) The quantized vector passed to the decoder; it equals \( e \) ("q" for quantized).
\( \operatorname{sg}[\cdot] \) Stop-gradient: passes the value through unchanged in the forward pass, but blocks gradients in the backward pass, so whatever is inside is treated as a constant.
\( \beta \) Commitment weight; the paper uses 0.25 and reports results robust from 0.1 to 2.0.

Why three terms are needed

  • Reconstruction trains the decoder directly, and the encoder through the straight-through copy of the gradient. It cannot train the codebook, because no gradient reaches \( e \).
  • Codebook loss fixes that. With \( z_e(x) \) frozen by the stop-gradient, it moves the chosen code vector \( e \) toward the encoder output, so codes learn to sit where the encoder's outputs land.
  • Commitment loss is the same distance with the stop-gradient on the other side. With \( e \) frozen, it pulls the encoder output toward its code, so the encoder commits to the codebook instead of drifting or growing without bound. \( \beta \) keeps this pull weaker than the codebook's.

Who learns from what: the decoder from reconstruction only, the encoder from reconstruction and commitment, the codebook from the codebook term only. Real codecs add three refinements on top. Many replace the codebook term with an exponential moving average (EMA), where each code vector becomes a running mean of the encoder outputs assigned to it. Quantizer dropout uses only the first \( n_q \) quantizers for each example, with \( n_q \) sampled at random, so one model serves many bitrates. And a discriminator, a second network trained to tell real audio from decoded audio, pushes the decoded audio toward sounding real, not just close on average. The codec also matches the discriminator's internal activations (a richer, steadier signal than its final verdict) and the spectrogram (see Mel spectrograms). SoundStream weights adversarial 1, feature matching 100 and spectral reconstruction 1.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Number and size of codebooks Bitrate and detail. EnCodec trains for 1.5 to 24 kbps with up to 32 codebooks; Mimi uses 8 of 2048 entries, SoundStream 1024 per codebook More codebooks, better quality, more tokens for the LM. Larger codebooks risk unused entries.
VQ or FSQ FSQ reports codebook usage near 100% with no auxiliary losses FSQ removes the collapse machinery. VQ gives a learned, adaptive codebook.
EMA codebook SoundStream updates codebooks by EMA (decay 0.99), starts them with k-means on the first batch and replaces codes that no frame uses Keeps codes in use; adds bookkeeping.
Commitment weight \( \beta \) VQ-VAE uses 0.25 and reports little change from 0.1 to 2.0 Too low lets the encoder drift; too high holds it to its codes.
Quantizer dropout probability Share of examples that use fewer quantizers (DAC: 0.5; Mimi quantizes only 50% of sequences) Dropout on every example hurt full-bandwidth quality in DAC.
Lookup space DAC looks codes up in 8 or 32 dimensions and L2-normalises, so distance becomes cosine similarity Keeps codes in use without k-means initialisation or random restarts.
Loss mix Mimi drops the reconstruction loss and keeps feature matching and adversarial losses Automatic scores fall (ViSQOL 1.84) but listening scores rise: MUSHRA gives 81.0 against 58.8 for an EnCodec-style mix. Both are explained in Judging a tokenizer.

What goes wrong

  • Codebook collapse. Many codes go unused, so the real bitrate falls below the nominal one. EMA with dead-code replacement, k-means starts, low-dimensional normalised lookup and FSQ all target this. Inference Track codebook usage per codebook during training.
  • Encoder drift. Without a commitment term, encoder outputs wander away from the codes they should match.
  • Chasing the wrong metric. Reconstruction scores can disagree with listening tests, as in Mimi.
  • Dropout everywhere. Applying quantizer dropout to every example costs full-bandwidth quality.

Rebuilding the audio is not the only goal. Why tokens should also be easy for the LM to predict is the subject of the next section. Model-by-model detail: How codecs are trained: VQ-VAE, RVQ and continuous latents.

In the interview, say:

An RVQ codec is a VQ-VAE-style autoencoder: the encoder output is snapped to a codebook with a straight-through gradient, trained with reconstruction, commitment and adversarial losses. Each quantizer codes the previous residual, and quantizer dropout makes one model serve many bitrates. The failure to watch is codebook collapse, which EMA with dead-code replacement or FSQ addresses.
