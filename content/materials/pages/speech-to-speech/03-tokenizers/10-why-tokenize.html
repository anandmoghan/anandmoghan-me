Why tokenize audio at all

A language model predicts the next item from a finite menu. Audio is the opposite: a continuous waveform with tens of thousands of numbers per second. Tokenizing audio means compressing it into a short sequence of items an LM can model the way it models text.

Think of sheet music. A page of symbols is a few hundred bytes, yet a pianist can recreate the sound from it. A tokenizer is sheet music learned from data, and the decoder is the pianist. The signal basics are in Sound as numbers.

How it works

The pipeline cuts the waveform into frames, short slices such as 80 ms (see Frames, hops and Hz). An encoder turns each frame into a latent, a vector of numbers that summarises the slice. A quantizer then replaces the latent with the closest entry of a codebook, a table of \( K \) learned vectors, and the code (the token) is that entry's row number. A codec (or tokenizer) does three jobs: it sets the sequence length the LM sees, it sets the ceiling on audio quality, and it decides how easy the LM's task is. The first two are arithmetic.

Each frame becomes one code per codebook. So the code rate is the frame rate times the number of codebooks, and the bitrate adds the bits per code. A codebook with \( K \) entries needs \( \log_2 K \) bits to name one entry.

\[ \text{codes/s} = f \times Q, \qquad \text{bitrate} = f \times Q \times \log_2 K \]

Here \( f \) is the frame rate and \( Q \) the number of codebooks. Take Mimi, the codec in Moshi: \( f = 12.5 \) Hz (one frame every 80 ms), \( Q = 8 \), \( K = 2048 \). A 2048-entry codebook needs 11 bits, so eight of them at 12.5 Hz cost \( 12.5 \times 8 \times 11 = 1100 \) bits per second, or 1.1 kbps.

The LM does not take 100 steps per second. Its main network takes one step per frame, 12.5 per second, and a small second network predicts that frame's 8 codes one after another. So the "100 per second" counts code predictions, while the expensive big-model steps number 12.5. Spoken English runs at about 3 to 4 text tokens per second, so 12.5 steps per second is a few times the text rate, not tens of times. That is why frame rate is the first number to set.

Tokens come in two forms: discrete codes, covered in the next sections, and continuous latents, covered in the last section. In images, video and audio alike, the tokenizer sets the quality ceiling more than the generator does.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Frame rate \( f \) LM steps per second. Mimi 12.5 Hz, CosyVoice 2 25 Hz, EnCodec 75 Hz, DAC about 86 Hz, VibeVoice 7.5 Hz (continuous) Lower rate means fewer autoregressive steps and a cheaper LM, at some cost in reconstruction and intelligibility. About 12.5 Hz is the common default.
Fixed or dynamic rate A fixed codec spends the same number of frames on silence and on fast speech. FlexiCodec merges neighbouring frames with similar content, so the average rate can be set between 3 Hz and 12.5 Hz at inference. Q-SPT replaces average pooling with learned queries that attend over the frames Fewer frames where nothing changes, at the cost of variable-length frames. Average pooling treats every frame alike and throws content away; Q-SPT adds a text-prediction loss to keep it.
Codebooks \( Q \) and size \( K \) Codes per frame and bits per code: Mimi 8 codebooks of 2048 entries; GLM-4-Voice and WavTokenizer use a single codebook More codebooks give more detail and more codes to model per frame. Larger codebooks carry more per code and are harder to keep fully used (see the next section).
Bitrate The product of the three above: Mimi 1.1 kbps, GLM-4-Voice 175 bps, ZipCodec 0.80 kbps at 6.25 Hz, TaDiCodec 87.5 bps The overall budget. Ultra-low rates lean on extra machinery: TaDiCodec at 6.25 Hz needs the text at decode time, and ZipCodec distils a large speech model (WavLM) during training and has 842M parameters (yet runs in real time on a consumer CPU).
Causal against non-causal Whether the encoder and decoder read future audio Mimi uses only causal convolutions, with 160 ms theoretical and 200 ms practical latency. Causality bounds the first-audio latency in a streaming system.

What goes wrong

  • A quality cliff at low rate. WavTokenizer scores PESQ 1.70 and UTMOS 3.60 at 40 tokens per second against 2.37 and 4.05 at 75 (both are automatic quality scores, explained in Judging a tokenizer). A controlled ablation reproduces a cliff at 6.25 Hz, then traces it to the training setup: a fixed clip duration gives too few tokens at low rates. After the fix, word error rate (WER, here measured on the decoded audio) degrades smoothly with phonemic load down to 3.1 Hz and 1.6 Hz. ZipCodec at 6.25 Hz reports better results than existing streaming codecs at comparable bitrates. Treat a cliff as a warning about your training configuration before you treat it as a floor.
  • More rate is not always better. Pooling X-Codec 2 from 50 Hz to 25 Hz raised UTMOSv2 on seven languages, for example Dutch from 2.168 to 2.457.
  • Extra inputs hide the cost. TaDiCodec reaches 6.25 Hz with WER 3.02, but removing the text input at decode causes significant degradation.

In the interview, say:

The tokenizer sets the sequence length and the quality ceiling before the LM does anything. Frame rate times codebooks gives codes per second, and I default to about 12.5 Hz because the big model then takes only a few steps per word; I would test lower or dynamic rates carefully, since reported cliffs are partly training artifacts.
