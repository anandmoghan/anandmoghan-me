Judging a tokenizer

"Which tokenizer is better?" has no single answer, because a tokenizer has three customers. The decoder wants to rebuild the sound faithfully. The language model wants tokens it can predict. The final product wants whatever the tokens make possible, such as accurate speech recognition or natural-sounding TTS.

Think of shorthand notes from a lecture. Good notes let you rewrite the lecture word for word, follow a clear pattern so the next line is easy to guess, and help you pass the exam. Notes can win on one count and lose on another, so a fair comparison checks all three.

How it works

Each question has its own family of measures, and this section is where the chapter defines them.

Question Typical measures What it misses
Does it rebuild the audio? (reconstruction) PESQ and ViSQOL (automatic scores that compare decoded audio with the original), UTMOS (a network that predicts the mean opinion score, MOS: the 1 to 5 score a listener would give), MUSHRA (a 0 to 100 listening test where people rate clips), WER of a recogniser run on the decoded audio Whether an LM can predict the tokens or use them for anything.
Can an LM predict it? (predictability) Perplexity of a small LM trained on the tokens; how stable the code index is for the same sound; coherence tests such as SALMon Whether the tokens are also high quality when decoded.
Is it useful? (downstream) Probe tasks (a small classifier trained on frozen tokens), ABX (does the representation keep different phonemes apart; lower error is better), full tasks such as recognition and TTS, as in benchmarks like DASB Cost: each result needs training something on top.

One benchmark packages these as four axes: reconstruction quality, codebook index stability, the perplexity of a decoder-only transformer trained on the tokens, and probe tasks. Its authors report that the measures correlate, so a cheap proxy can sometimes stand in for an expensive one. A separate analysis compares the statistics of the tokens of 13 codecs across architectures, corpora and noise conditions, a reminder that "predictable" depends on the data as well as the codec.

Nominal against entropy-coded bitrate

The bitrate so far is the nominal one (frame rate times codebooks times bits per code). It assumes every code is equally likely. Real codes are not. A skewed code is cheaper to send, because frequent values can be given short names, the way Morse code gives the common letter E a single dot. The entropy-coded rate uses the real probabilities:

\[ \text{nominal} = f \, Q \log_2 K, \qquad \text{entropy-coded} = f \sum_{i=1}^{Q} H_i, \qquad H_i = -\sum_k p_i(k) \log_2 p_i(k) \]

Here \( p_i(k) \) is how often entry \( k \) of codebook \( i \) occurs, and \( H_i \) is that codebook's entropy in bits. Example: a 4-entry codebook (2 bits nominal) whose entries have probabilities 0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1 has \( H = 0.7 \times 0.51 + 3 \times 0.1 \times 3.32 \approx 1.36 \) bits. The same quality costs about two thirds of the nominal rate.

A codec that entropy-codes its output and one that sends fixed-size codes are not on equal footing at the same nominal bitrate. A recent speech codec pairs scalar quantization with a learned entropy model and skips residual symbols that are highly predictable. Its authors report average bitrate savings at equal quality (the BD-rate) of 39.9% on ViSQOL and 76.3% on PESQ over two test sets. Codebook collapse is the same effect by accident: unused codes make the real rate lower than the nominal one.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Which measure to optimise Reconstruction only, or also LM-facing losses (future-token prediction, audio-text alignment) LM-facing losses report both better coherence and better reconstruction in one codec, but add training cost.
Listening tests against automatic scores Mimi scores lower on automatic measures but higher on MUSHRA (figures in Codec mechanics) Listening tests are slow and costly; automatic scores can mislead.
Predictability probe Train a small LM on the tokens and read off perplexity Cheap and fast; reflects the training corpus as much as the codec.
Downstream probe Use the tokens for recognition or TTS Closest to the product; costly and task-specific.
Rate accounting Compare at equal nominal rate, or at equal entropy-coded rate Equal entropy-coded rate is fairer; entropy coding adds a model and some latency.

What goes wrong

  • One metric decides everything. Reconstruction-best tokens are not LM-best: acoustic detail adds uncertainty the LM must absorb. Mimi's objective scores fall while its listening score rises.
  • Nominal rates compared across codecs. A fixed-rate codec and an entropy-coded one need the same accounting before the numbers mean anything.
  • Results that do not travel. Token statistics shift with corpus and noise, so a codec ranked on clean read speech may rank differently on phone calls. Inference Test on audio like your product's.

In the interview, say:

I judge a tokenizer on three counts: reconstruction, how predictable the tokens are for the LM, and how well they work downstream. I compare bitrates after entropy coding, not just nominal, and I trust a listening test over a single automatic score.
