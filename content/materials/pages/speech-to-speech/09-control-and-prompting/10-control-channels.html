Control channels

A speech model has to settle four things at once: what to say, who says it, how they say it, and how long it takes. Each one enters the model through its own channel. The words arrive as text, the speaker as a reference clip, the delivery as an instruction or tags, and the timing as a duration target.

Think of directing an actor. You hand over the script, cast the person, whisper a note ("softer, a little sad"), mark a pause in the margin, and say the scene must fit thirty seconds. A model takes the same kinds of direction, and reads each one in a different place.

How it works

The voice reference works by continuation. The codes of a short clip sit in the context, and the model carries on in that voice, with no fixed speaker embedding. A natural-language instruction ("whisper, slow, British accent") is text placed next to the content, often followed by an end-of-prompt token, or fed as a separate stream. Inline tags, such as a bracketed laugh or a pause element, are part of the spoken text and control one event at one point. Duration control gives the model an explicit token count for exact timing, or a free mode that keeps the prompt's natural prosody. Numeric fields, such as a speed multiplier, sit outside the model's wording entirely.

The classifier-free guidance from continuous autoregression (formula in Levers) can also be applied to the style or text condition, which makes it a strength knob for style. Inference

A reference clip carries timbre and delivery together, so cloning a voice also clones its emotion. Teams separate the two so that timbre can come from one clip and emotion from another, possibly a different speaker. One method adds a speaker classifier that tries to guess the speaker from the style representation. A gradient reversal layer flips its gradient, so the encoder learns to remove whatever the classifier uses and the style vector carries no timbre. Another method skips representation tricks and trains on synthetic pairs: the same sentence in clearly different emotions, so the contrast is obvious and the model can learn an edit from it.

Two refinements work after the base model is trained. A style planner is a text model that reads the dialogue so far and the reply, and writes the instruction for the synthesiser. Candidate instructions are scored by how likely the target speech tokens are under a frozen synthesiser, and GRPO (the sample-and-compare update from streaming ASR latency) trains the planner on that score. The authors found that matching a caption to the audio only weakly predicts what a given synthesiser will produce, which is why the planner is tuned against the synthesiser itself. For non-verbal tags such as laughter or a breath, one post-training method uses a frozen recogniser that can transcribe such sounds as the reward. It passes the reward gradient back through the flow sampler into the language model and acoustic head, and follows tags better than supervised fine-tuning, with penalties that keep speaker similarity and quality.

One trap: a speed setting that changes playback rate does not change how the model composes speech. A convincingly faster delivery needs an instruction as well.

What goes wrong

The biggest risk is leakage. The mechanism that copies timbre also copies emotion, background noise and microphone channel, so a noisy prompt makes the output inherit the room. Detect it with emotion-embedding similarity between prompt and output plus a noise-floor check. Gate prompts at upload (reject clipped, noisy or reverberant audio, or clean it first) and prefer neutral, clean references when the script sets the style.

Tags and instructions fail in quieter ways. A tag for a delivery the voice was never trained on lands unreliably, and a prompt can steer pacing and accent but cannot fully replace voice design. Delivery guides left in the text may be read aloud, so put them in the style field and keep the text field to spoken content. Pause tools give fixed durations, so a flat read often needs a script edit rather than a control change. Inference

Cloning someone without consent is the central misuse risk. Gate cloning on a consent step, such as reading a randomly generated passcode or recording a consent statement. Marking outputs and tracing clips is covered in Safety and provenance.

Evaluate control with emotion similarity from one fixed emotion encoder and speaker similarity from one fixed speaker encoder. Scores from different encoders do not compare.

In the interview, say:

"I treat control as separate channels: a reference clip for identity, an instruction or tags for emotion, and a duration mode for sync. I keep emotion disentangled from timbre so style transfers across speakers, and I gate references for noise and consent."
