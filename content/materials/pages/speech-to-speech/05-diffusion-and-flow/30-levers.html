Levers and optimisations: trading steps, guidance and size

A flow model is a compute budget. Every utterance costs a number of network passes, and guidance doubles that number. Each lever below either spends fewer passes or makes each pass count for more.

Some levers tune the sampler and need no retraining: step count, solver, step schedule and guidance. Others change the model: size, target space and distillation. The rest change how it is deployed: streaming masks (next section) and reward training (the section after).

How the cost adds up

Cost is NFE (the number of function evaluations, see The idea) times passes per step times network size. Guidance runs the network once with the conditions and once without, so it roughly doubles the cost. Inference Papers differ on whether the reported NFE already counts both guidance passes, so compare across papers with care.

Solver. Euler takes a step along the current arrow. Midpoint peeks halfway along, re-reads the arrow there, and uses that one instead. That costs two evaluations per step but follows curves better. Voicebox's default of 16 midpoint steps is 32 evaluations; F5-TTS uses plain Euler at 16 or 32.

Step schedule. Steps need not be evenly spaced. Sway Sampling warps the time grid so more steps fall early, where the model sketches the contour of the speech. It draws \( u \) uniformly from \( [0,1] \) and maps it to a time.

\[ t = u + s\Big(\cos\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}u\big) - 1 + u\Big), \qquad s = -1 \text{ by default} \]

Negative \( s \) pushes the grid toward small \( t \). With \( s=-1 \), the middle of the even grid, \( u=0.5 \), becomes \( t = 0.5 - (\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{4}) - 1 + 0.5) \approx 0.29 \), and the grid \( u = 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 \) becomes \( t \approx 0.08, 0.29, 0.62 \). Inference These values come from applying the formula by hand; they match the sway-sampled times listed for a 4-step student in one of the sources. The method needs no retraining and costs nothing at run time; a cosine time schedule in CosyVoice 2 gives the same kind of early-heavy spacing.

Guidance. Classifier-free guidance (first met in Continuous autoregression) runs the network with and without the conditions, then pushes the result away from the unconditional answer. The scale \( w \) sets how hard.

\[ \hat v = v_\theta(x,t,c) + w\,\big(v_\theta(x,t,c) - v_\theta(x,t,\varnothing)\big) \]

A higher \( w \) makes the output follow the text and voice more tightly. Reported values run from about 0.7 to 2 across models, with different conventions, so they are not directly comparable. Since guidance doubles the cost, several recent methods try to remove the second pass; they appear under distillation.

Distillation: a teacher trains a faster student

Distillation trains a second, faster network (the student) to reproduce the results of a slow, good network (the teacher). The teacher might need 32 steps; the student is trained so that 4 steps, or even 1, give nearly the same picture.

Fewer steps lose quality because real paths bend (crossing paths). A 32-step teacher re-reads its arrow 32 times and corrects its course; one step must jump the whole distance along an arrow that points toward the average of many possible speech samples. The result is smooth and blurry. Every method below answers the same question: how to teach the student to land on one clean sample anyway.

Inference: where to start

For a flow decoder, step distillation is the biggest latency lever, and recent work shows a 1-evaluation student can match its 32-evaluation teacher on word error rate. The cheapest quality lever is the schedule, because it is free. Guidance is the one to tune last, since it both costs compute and has a sharp optimum.

What goes wrong

  • Over-smoothing at low step counts. Inference Speech sounds muffled or mushy. Sweep NFE against word error rate, speaker similarity and real-time factor to find the knee.
  • Guidance too high. Inference The output follows the text but sounds harsh or exaggerated, and diversity drops. Sweep the scale on a held-out set and listen; do not tune it on word error rate alone.
  • Distilled students that look fine on one metric. Inference Check intelligibility, speaker similarity and naturalness together, and compare against the teacher.
  • Quoting latency without a definition. Papers mix theoretical and measured numbers, and "first packet" means different things. Measure your own system end to end.
In the interview, say:

"Cost is steps times guidance passes times network size. I would spend the free levers first (schedule, solver), then distil to two or three steps and fold guidance into the student, and I would sweep every change against word error rate, speaker similarity and a listening test."
