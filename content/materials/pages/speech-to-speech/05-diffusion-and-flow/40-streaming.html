Streaming a flow decoder: chunks, look-ahead and first-packet latency

A flow model normally paints the whole canvas before showing it. That is fine for an audiobook and fatal for a conversation, where the listener expects sound within a fraction of a second.

The fix is to paint in strips. Tokens arrive from the language model in small chunks, the flow decoder renders each chunk as soon as it can, and finished audio flows out while later chunks are still being written. The cost is that each strip must join smoothly with its neighbours.

How it works

Chunk-wise causal masks. In an ordinary flow decoder every frame can look at every other frame. For streaming, a frame sees the past and only a short window of the future. CosyVoice 2 trains one model with four mask types picked at random per example (fully non-causal, fully causal, past plus a few future chunks, and a wider version of the same), so one model serves both streaming and offline use.

Look-ahead. A chunk decoded with no knowledge of what follows tends to end badly, because the right context shapes the last frames. Look-ahead lets each chunk peek at tokens from the next one. CosyVoice 2 adds a right-padded convolution layer before the up-sampling for this reason, and Kimi-Audio uses chunk-wise causal masking with look-ahead to fix chunk-boundary issues.

Few steps without losing the streaming. The usual way to cut the per-chunk solve is a mean-flow decoder, which learns the average arrow over an interval (distillation). At one or two steps it tends to struggle to keep acoustic quality. X-Pred MeanFlow changes the output: the network predicts the mel itself, a direct acoustic target, and the average arrow is computed from that prediction by a formula. It also blocks attention in chunks, in selected layers only, so each chunk sees a bounded context. Its abstract reports better few-step token-to-mel quality than a baseline that predicts the average arrow directly, and stable streaming, but gives no numbers.

First-packet latency is the time from the request to the first audio packet. Inference For a hybrid stack it is roughly the time for the language model to produce its first chunk (plus look-ahead tokens), plus the flow solve for that chunk, plus the vocoder. The flow solve is the part this chapter's levers shrink: fewer steps means a shorter bar for each decoder box in the figure.

Self-reported figures show the range, though definitions differ. One 2-step distilled decoder reports 325 ms first packet, where its own table lists 843 ms for a 10-step baseline. Another system reports 97 ms, and a continuous-token system reports 85 ms and 54 ms in two streaming modes after distillation. Do not rank them against each other.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Chunk size How much audio each decoder call covers Inference Small chunks give a lower first packet but more joins and more overhead
Look-ahead length How much future context each chunk sees Smoother chunk boundaries, but each look-ahead token adds waiting time
Mask mix in training Whether one model supports streaming and offline Uniform sampling over four masks gives one model for both; the causal masks are weaker than the full one (Inference)
Steps per chunk Solve time for each chunk Few-step distillation gives the largest cut; predicting the mel directly targets quality at one or two steps
Token rate and multi-token prediction How much audio each token covers; several tokens per language-model pass Both speed the language-model half of the pipeline, not the flow solve; lower rates give a coarser time grid
Causal vocoder or codec decoder Whether the final stage can stream Must carry state between chunks; see tokens to waveform

What goes wrong

  • Artefacts at chunk boundaries. Inference Clicks, a pitch jump or a change in timbre where one chunk ends and the next begins. Listen at the joins, and compare streaming output to the same input decoded offline.
  • Streaming quality gap. Inference The causal model is usually a little worse than the non-causal one. Measure word error rate and speaker similarity for both modes.
  • Latency hidden in look-ahead. Look-ahead tokens must exist before a chunk can be decoded, so they add to first-packet latency even though the flow solve is short.
  • Unfair latency comparisons. Theoretical and measured numbers differ, and papers define the clock differently.
In the interview, say:

"To stream a flow decoder I train with chunk-wise causal masks and a little look-ahead, so each chunk renders as tokens arrive. I budget first-packet latency per stage; for the renderer the lever is the step count, so I would distil to two or three steps."
