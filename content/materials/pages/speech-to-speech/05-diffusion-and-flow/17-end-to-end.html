End to end: a speech LLM with a diffusion head, from data to audio

This section walks one system through its whole life: building the audio tokenizer, training the LLM and its diffusion head, and generating speech. It follows the continuous-latent design (an LLM whose output layer is a small diffusion or flow head), with a 10-second clip as the running example. The token-then-flow alternative comes at the end.

The latent's life cycle

Latents appear at every stage, but they come from different places and play different roles. This table is the whole story in one view; the phases below fill in the detail.

Stage Where the latent comes from How it is used
A. Tokenizer training The VAE encoder on real audio: mean and spread per frame, sampled as \( \mu + \sigma\epsilon \) The VAE decoder rebuilds the audio from it; the KL term keeps the latent space tidy
B. LLM training, inputs The frozen VAE encoder on real training audio: the true latents of the clip Fed into the LLM as sequence positions after the text (teacher forcing)
B. LLM training, targets The true next latent at each position, from the same encoder Noised and given to the head, which must predict the noise; never generated here
C. Inference, voice prompt The frozen VAE encoder on a few seconds of reference audio A prefix in the sequence, so the LLM continues in that voice
C. Inference, generated The head, cleaning pure noise in a few steps, conditioned on \( h \) Fed back as the next input; all generated latents go to the frozen VAE decoder for audio

The LLM never outputs a mean or spread in this design; those belong to the VAE. Designs with an explicit Gaussian head are the exception (How the hidden state steers the head).

Phase A: build the audio tokenizer

A variational autoencoder is trained on audio alone, before any LLM exists (Continuous latents explains the mechanics). Its encoder describes each short frame with a mean \( \mu \) and a spread \( \sigma \); during training the latent is sampled as \( \mu + \sigma\epsilon \) and the decoder must rebuild the audio from it. One speech system fixes \( \sigma \) in advance to avoid variance collapse (Inference a spread shrunk toward zero stops regularising the latent space, which ends up rough and hard to model). It uses a causal encoder that downsamples 24 kHz audio 3200 times to 7.5 frames per second, with codec-style reconstruction and adversarial losses. Audio autoencoders of this kind keep the KL term very small, for example a weight of \( 10^{-4} \). When training ends, encoder and decoder are frozen: a compressor and a decompressor.

Phase B: train the LLM and its diffusion head

Data and model. Every training clip is paired with its transcript and run through the frozen VAE encoder. A 10-second clip at 7.5 frames per second becomes 75 latent vectors (derived). The backbone is a pretrained text LLM; one system uses 1.5B and 7B Qwen2.5 models. The sequence is the transcript followed by one position per latent frame. Text positions get their vectors from the usual embedding table; latent positions get theirs from the latent vector itself, and attention runs over the whole sequence as usual. 

position:  1    2    3   ...  20   21   22   23  ...  95
input:    [the][cat][sat] ... [.]  [x1] [x2] [x3] ... [x75]
           text token embeddings    latent vectors, one per audio frame

Inference In practice a latent must be mapped to the LLM's hidden width, typically by a small projection; the papers we draw on do not spell this layer out.

Loss, at every latent position. The LLM produces a hidden state \( h \) summarising the text and the latents so far. The true next latent \( x \) is mixed with fresh Gaussian noise at a random level, and the diffusion head, conditioned on \( h \), predicts that noise (or a flow velocity). The error trains the head and flows back through \( h \) into the LLM, so the two learn together while the tokenizer stays frozen. This is teacher forcing: the LLM always reads true latents in training. One patch-based system also learns a stop signal and drops the LLM's condition about one time in ten so that guidance works at inference. 

# Phase B, one training step (illustrative pseudocode)
text_ids, audio = batch
x = frozen_vae_encoder(audio)                 # [T, D] latents, e.g. T = 75 for 10 s
h = llm(text_ids, x[:-1])                     # one hidden state per latent position
t = random_noise_level(T)
eps = randn_like(x)
x_noisy = mix(x, eps, t)                      # schedule or straight-line mix
loss = mse(head(x_noisy, t, cond=h), eps)     # predict the noise (or the velocity)
loss.backward()                               # updates head AND llm; vae stays frozen

Inside the head: conditioning and making one latent

In training, no latent is generated: the true latent is the answer, and the head learns to clean up a corrupted copy of it. In generation, the latent is built from pure noise in a few cleaning steps. The same mechanism serves both.

How the condition gets in. The head takes \( h \) in through adaptive layer norm, as explained in How the hidden state steers the head. What matters here: the normalised activations are computed from the noisy latent, so \( h \) does not become the latent; it reshapes how every layer processes the noisy input.

Training feeds the head a noisy copy of the true latent and compares its predicted noise with the true noise. Generation starts from pure noise, reuses the same \( h \) at every step, and uses each predicted noise to clean the sample a little. Classifier-free guidance (introduced in Continuous autoregression) adds a second run of the head with a blank condition each step; this is why the condition is dropped for a fraction of training steps.

Phase C: generate speech

  1. Write the text, and optionally encode a few seconds of a reference voice into latents as a prefix.
  2. Run the LLM to get the hidden state \( h \) for the next position.
  3. Draw fresh noise the size of one latent and let the head denoise it in a few steps, guided by \( h \) (one speech system uses 10 solver steps and a guidance scale of about 1.3).
  4. Append the new latent to the sequence and repeat until the stop signal fires.
  5. Pass all generated latents through the frozen VAE decoder to get the waveform.
# Phase C, generation (illustrative pseudocode)
seq = [text_ids, vae_encoder(voice_prompt)]   # optional voice prefix
latents = []
while not stopped(seq):
    h = llm(seq)[-1]                          # hidden state for the next frame
    z = randn(D)                              # fresh noise, one latent's size
    for k in range(K):                        # e.g. K = 10 denoising steps
        z = head_step(z, k, cond=h)           # with classifier-free guidance
    latents.append(z); seq.append(z)          # feed the new latent back
audio = vae_decoder(latents)

For the 10-second example: 75 LLM steps and, at 10 head steps each, 750 small head evaluations (derived). Inference With a causal decoder, latents can be decoded and played in chunks as they arrive, which is how such a model would stream.

The alternative: tokens, then a flow decoder

The other common design keeps the LLM discrete. The token is never turned into audio directly: it becomes a conditioning signal, and a flow decoder generates the mel spectrogram from noise. One widely used system works like this.

  1. The speech tokenizer. A quantizer is placed inside a speech-recognition encoder and trained with the recognition objective, so its tokens carry mostly content (what is said, and when) rather than voice. It produces 25 tokens per second from a codebook of 6,561 codes.
  2. The LLM. It reads the text and predicts the next speech-token id with a softmax, trained with cross-entropy exactly like text; its output is 25 integers per second of speech.
  3. From token to condition. Each id goes through an embedding table to become a vector, and the vectors are upsampled by two to the 50 Hz frame rate of the mel, giving one conditioning vector per mel frame.
  4. The flow decoder. It starts from noise shaped like the whole output mel, whose length is fixed by the token count (10 seconds gives 250 tokens and 500 mel frames, derived), so the LLM sets the duration. Conditioned on the upsampled tokens (content and timing), a speaker embedding from a separate speaker model (who), and the voice prompt's mel as an unmasked prefix (exact voice), it turns the noise into a mel in about 10 steps; a vocoder makes the waveform.

Training differs from the continuous design: cross-entropy for the LLM, and a flow-matching loss for the decoder on real token and mel pairs with most of the mel masked so it learns to infill, described as separate objectives rather than end to end. Inference The split lets the LLM stay a normal LLM (softmax, sampling, cross-entropy, token-level RL) while the flow decoder supplies what coarse content tokens leave out: timbre, fine prosody and recording character. How the decoder is trained for cloning and run is in How a flow TTS model is trained and run.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Latent frame rate How many LLM steps a second of audio costs Lower rate is cheaper and fits long audio; the tokenizer must pack more into each frame
Frozen tokenizer Keeps the representation fixed while the generator trains Stable training; the tokenizer's quality caps the output
Head size and steps Cost per frame A small head with few steps keeps per-frame latency low; distillation recovers quality
Guidance scale How strictly the head follows \( h \) Higher is more faithful but costs extra passes and can over-sharpen
Noise scale (temperature) Variety between samples Lower is more stable and intelligible; higher adds speaker and prosody variety
Backbone size Content quality and context handling Larger is better and slower

Inference Several trade-off cells are engineering reasoning; measured effects are in the Levers section.

What goes wrong

  • Inference Exposure drift: the LLM trains on true latents but generates from its own, so errors can compound.
  • Inference The tokenizer caps quality: anything its decoder cannot rebuild, the whole system cannot produce.
  • RL is harder than for token models, because the head has no closed-form probability (Rewards).
In the interview, say:

"First train a VAE that compresses audio into a few continuous latents per second, then freeze it. Train an LLM on transcript plus latents to predict each next latent, with a small diffusion head conditioned on the hidden state in place of a softmax, end to end through that hidden state. At inference the head denoises fresh noise into each next latent, feeds it back, and the frozen VAE decoder turns the latents into audio."
