How a flow TTS model is trained and run

Think of a fill-in-the-blank test. Show the model a sentence of text and a recording of that sentence with a stretch blanked out, and ask it to paint the missing audio. Train on enough recordings and it learns two things: how text maps to sound, and how one speaker's voice stays consistent across a clip.

Voice cloning then comes for free. Give the model a few seconds of a new speaker, the transcript of those seconds, and the new text you want spoken. The blank is everything after the clip, and the model continues in the same voice. No separate speaker-recognition model has to be bolted on.

How it works

The model paints a mel spectrogram, not a waveform; a vocoder turns that into sound at the end (Decoders and vocoders). The figure shows both phases.

Training, step by step.

  1. Take a recording and compute its mel spectrogram.
  2. Hide a span of frames, typically 70 to 100% of them; the rest stay visible as context.
  3. Line up the text with the audio. A simple recipe pads the character sequence with filler tokens up to the mel length and lets the network work out which sound goes where. The older recipe expands phones to frame level using forced-alignment durations.
  4. Mix the hidden span with noise at a random flow time \( t \), as in the idea.
  5. The network sees text, visible audio, the noisy span and \( t \), and predicts the velocity. The loss counts only the hidden frames.
  6. Occasionally drop the audio context, or the audio and text together (F5-TTS uses probabilities of 0.3 and 0.2). This teaches the unconditional case that guidance needs later.

Inference. Put the reference clip first as the visible context, append noise for the part to generate, run the solver for \( N \) steps with guidance, and hand the mel to the vocoder. There is no explicit speaker embedding in this setup; the clip is the voice.

Fixing the length first

A non-autoregressive model paints every frame at once, so it must know the output length before it starts (duration and alignment basics). The noise tensor's size is the duration, and the chosen total doubles as a speech-rate dial: a shorter total means faster speech.

How duration is decided Idea Catch
A separate regression model A small network predicts how long each phone lasts, and the sum fixes the length One more model to train and keep in sync
A ratio estimate Scale the prompt's length by the ratio of text lengths (F5-TTS); a related recipe multiplies the phone count by the prompt's speaking rate Crude: it assumes the new text is spoken at the prompt's pace
Tokens fix it In the hybrid below, the language model's tokens already set the length Needs the language model and its latency
A predictor tuned with reinforcement learning Predict the remaining length with a classifier, then reward lengths that make the final speech score well Needs reward models and a training run

Inference The F5-TTS paper does not spell out which prompt quantity the ratio scales; the natural reading is the prompt's audio duration.

Tuning the predictor with rewards. DMOSpeech 2 treats duration as a decision worth optimising. Its predictor outputs a probability over 300 length bins of 100 ms each, for the time remaining. In training it draws a group of 16 candidate lengths, renders speech for each with the fast student model, and scores the result with a speech-recognition score and speaker similarity; lengths that led to better speech become more likely (the group method is in the rewards section). Removing this step raised English word error rate from 1.752 to 3.750 in the paper's ablation. Inference An average-length guess is often wrong for any one sentence: too short clips the words, too long invents silence.

Keeping alignment with fewer steps. A one-step student has no second chance to fix its timing. A recent flow-map method adds a soft-DTW refinement: dynamic time warping lines up two sequences that run at different speeds, and the soft version is smooth enough to train through. Inference The loss forgives small timing shifts but still punishes content in the wrong place.

The hybrid: tokens decide, flow renders. Many speech-to-speech systems keep an autoregressive language model for content and use flow only for acoustics, as in CosyVoice 2. Because the tokens already fix the content and its length, the hybrid needs no duration estimate (walk-through).

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Text alignment: filler padding or forced alignment Whether a separate aligner and phone conversion are needed Filler padding simplifies the pipeline; the network must learn alignment itself
Duration source Output length and speech rate An extra model against a crude heuristic against a reward-tuned model that needs a training run
Hidden fraction in training How much the network must invent from text alone Hiding 70 to 100% matches the inference situation of a short prompt and a long blank
AR tokens in front of the flow model Content and length come from the language model; flow only renders acoustics Needs the language model and its latency; keeps content tight
Backbone size Quality per forward pass and cost per step The common open models sit around 335M parameters; smaller is faster

What goes wrong

  • Duration errors. Inference Too short rushes or clips the speech; too long adds trailing silence or stretched words. Check speech rate and the end of the clip across a test set.
  • Speaker leakage. Inference Because the prompt is context, anything in it can be continued: room noise, a hurried delivery, an emotional tone. Choose clean, neutral prompts, and compare speaker similarity against the reference as well as listening.
  • Bandwidth ceiling. A mel model cannot exceed what its vocoder and training data contain. Codec research warns that models trained on band-limited source data often fail to reconstruct content above the data's true sampling rate.
  • A good metric that hides a defect. Word error rate measures intelligibility, not naturalness. Report it with speaker similarity and a listening test.
In the interview, say:

"Flow TTS is masked infilling: the model learns the arrow from noise to the hidden mel, given text and the audio around the gap, and zero-shot cloning is just infilling after a reference clip. In speech-to-speech stacks it is usually the acoustic renderer behind an autoregressive content model."
