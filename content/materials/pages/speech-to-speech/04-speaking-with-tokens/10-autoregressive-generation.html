Autoregressive speech generation

A text LLM writes by predicting the next word again and again. Once speech is a sequence of discrete codes (see Tokenizers), a speech LM does the same with codec tokens instead of words: read everything so far, predict one more token, append it, repeat.

Think of finishing someone's sentence in their own voice. The model is handed the words to say and a short sample of the target voice, and it continues the audio. There is no separate "voice" input; the voice is part of the sequence being continued.

How it works

The loop, in order:

  1. Build the prefix: the text to speak, then the voice prompt (codes of a short reference clip), then a start marker.
  2. The LM reads the sequence and outputs a probability distribution over the codec vocabulary (a couple of thousand codes per codebook is typical).
  3. A sampler picks one code, which is appended to the context.
  4. This repeats one frame at a time (about 12.5 frames per second is a common rate) until the model emits a stop token.
  5. The codec decoder turns the generated codes into a waveform.

Prompts act as the voice. Because the reference clip sits inside the context, the model clones the speaker by continuing the sequence, with no fine-tuning and no speaker embedding. The same mechanism also copies pace, emotion and room noise; that cost returns in Control and prompting.

Alignment is learned, not given. Alignment means knowing, at every moment of the audio, which word of the text is being voiced. Picture a finger moving along the sentence as the speech plays: for "turn left now" it sits on "turn" for the first third of the audio, then slides to "left", then to "now". Nothing hands the LM that finger. A model with separate text and audio streams can learn it in cross-attention (layers that let audio positions look at text positions); a decoder-only model such as Llasa learns it inside ordinary self-attention over one long sequence. Either way, nothing in the loss forces the finger to move forward only, which is the root of most failures below.

Content versus acoustics. The dominant high-quality recipe lets the AR model decide content and prosody, then hands the tokens to a renderer that restores acoustic detail (see Diffusion and flow and Tokens to waveform).

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Temperature, top-k, top-p How adventurous the sampler is Higher is livelier prosody but more bad takes; lower is stable but flatter
Best-of-N reranking and inference-time search Sample several takes, or guide decoding with speech-understanding verifiers, and keep the best N times the compute, or more latency; the score (ASR error plus speaker similarity) defines "best"
Voice prompt length and quality How well the voice is captured A clean prompt helps; a noisy one is copied faithfully
Codec frame rate Steps per second of audio Lower rate is faster and fits longer audio in context, but each token carries more
Alignment loss Pushes attention to move monotonically: a CTC-style loss (see Audio meets text) or an attention prior, a fixed band along the diagonal that keeps attention near the expected position Adds no parameters and reduces skipping and repeating

Sampling is the cheapest lever, and reranking is the most reliable one. Pushing the reranking signal into training (online RL) invites reward hacking (guards): one report finds that RL on transcription signals lowers error rates but collapses prosody into monotone speech.

What goes wrong

  • Repeating, skipping, babbling. The attention loses its place, so words repeat, vanish or turn into noise, and hallucination correlates with model uncertainty. Teams measure it as word error rate (WER, defined in The speech encoder) or its character version, from one fixed, strong ASR model, and fix it with monotonic alignment losses, attention priors and reranking.
  • Stop-token failure. Sampled late, the model babbles on; sampled early, the clip is cut off. Inference Check clip length against text length, and cap generation at a multiple of the expected duration.
  • Long-form drift. Over minutes, errors compound and nothing re-anchors the voice. Inference Measure speaker similarity in sliding windows against the first window, and synthesise in chunks that each reuse the original reference.
  • Sampling instability. Some seeds are bad while the average looks fine. Track the failure rate across seeds, lower the temperature, and rerank.
  • Duration guessed wrong. Non-autoregressive flow models cannot stall, but they must choose the total duration up front, and that choice can be wrong (see How flow TTS works).
In the interview, say:

"Speech LMs are next-token predictors over codec tokens, with the voice supplied as a prefix of reference codes. The alignment between text and audio is learned by attention, so the characteristic failures are repeated or skipped words and runaway generation. I would fix them with monotonic alignment losses and best-of-N reranking scored by ASR error and speaker similarity."
