Streaming generation

A voice agent cannot wait for the full reply before it starts talking. Streaming generation means text arrives in pieces and audio leaves in small packets, so the first sound plays while the rest is still being produced.

Think of a simultaneous interpreter. They start speaking after hearing a few words and stay slightly behind the speaker. Start too early and the intonation is guessed; start too late and the listener waits.

How it works

There are three common ways to couple text and speech in one streaming model.

Pattern How text and audio line up Typical use
Full text, then packets All text is known; audio streams out in small packets Sentence-level TTS with a causal codec
Interleaved One sequence: N text tokens, then M speech tokens, repeating TTS fed by another LLM's streamed output; spoken dialogue models
Parallel streams Text and audio on one clock as separate streams, offset by a chosen delay Duplex models

Interleaving ratio. A ratio such as 13 text tokens to 26 speech tokens describes the mix, not a duration. Only the speech token rate turns it into seconds: at 12.5 frames per second, 26 speech tokens are about two seconds of audio (arithmetic from the stated rate). Dialogue models fix the ratio so the text stays slightly ahead of the speech.

Text leads speech. A TTS needs a little text lookahead before it commits prosody, just as a streaming ASR needs a little audio lookahead before it commits a word (see Look-ahead). In delayed-streams models this is a per-stream setting: delay the text for ASR, delay the audio for TTS. One model using incremental text can start speaking after only two text tokens, against five for an earlier interleaved model, with a much shorter first packet.

First packet. With a causal codec at 12.5 Hz, a packet of 4 tokens covers 320 ms, and a small model can emit it in under 100 ms of LM time plus a few milliseconds of decoding. A chunk-wise causal flow decoder also waits for a few look-ahead tokens from the next chunk before decoding each chunk. Multi-token prediction (emitting about ten audio tokens per forward pass) cuts the number of passes; one system reports its first chunk dropping from 236 ms to 53 ms. Compare shapes, not digits: these figures come from different hardware and definitions.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Text:speech interleave ratio and text lead How much text the model sees before and between speech chunks, and how far text runs ahead of audio More text or lead gives better prosody planning but a later first sound
Packet size Tokens decoded per audio packet Small first packet is fast; tiny packets raise overhead and risk seams
Decoder look-ahead Future tokens the renderer may see per chunk Hides chunk boundaries, delays the first chunk
Tokens per forward pass (MTP) Number of LM passes per second of audio Faster; needs an extra module trained to predict ahead
Frame rate and causal codec AR steps per second; whether future audio is needed Lower rate is faster; fewer codes per second of detail
Renderer steps (flow decoder) Network evaluations per chunk Fewer steps cut latency; see Levers for flow

For flow-based renderers, step distillation is the biggest latency lever; on the AR side, a lower token rate plus a causal codec is. Budget first-packet latency per stage, not as one number.

What goes wrong

  • Reading a ratio as a duration. "13:26" says nothing about seconds until you know the token rate, and first-chunk latency is not steady-state delay.
  • Speech outruns text. If the upstream LLM streams slower than the speech consumes, the model has to pause or guess. Inference Keep a small text buffer and fill with a short filler.
  • Committing prosody too early. With no text lead, intonation is guessed from too little context. Inference
  • Chunk seams. Clicks or prosody jumps appear at packet boundaries. Mitigate with overlap, look-ahead and crossfade, and measure boundary discontinuity next to first-packet time.
  • Incomparable numbers. Reported latencies use different hardware, test sets and definitions of "first packet". Re-measure on your own stack.
In the interview, say:

"I budget first-packet latency per stage. For the LM the levers are token rate, interleave ratio and multi-token prediction; for the renderer it is look-ahead and step count. A text:speech ratio only becomes a duration through the speech token rate, so I always quote both."
