Post-training: preference and distillation

Pretraining teaches a model to speak; post-training teaches it which replies are better. A coach does not rewrite your technique; they watch two attempts and say which one was closer. Preference methods work the same way, learning from pairs of responses where one is marked as better.

Speech adds one wrinkle. A reply is both words and sound, and the two do not behave the same under preference optimisation.

How it works

DPO on text tokens. Direct preference optimisation (DPO) trains on pairs: one reply is preferred, the other rejected. The loss raises the likelihood of the preferred reply relative to the rejected one. "Relative" matters: both likelihoods are compared with a frozen reference model, a copy from before DPO started. The reference anchors the model, so it cannot win by drifting far from where it began. The distance from the reference is measured with the KL divergence from Continuous latents.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\log \sigma\!\left(\beta\left[\log\frac{\pi(y_w)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)} - \log\frac{\pi(y_l)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l)}\right]\right) \]

Here \( y_w \) is the preferred reply, \( y_l \) the rejected one, \( \pi \) the model being trained, \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) the frozen reference, and \( \beta \) a strength setting. Each bracketed term says how much more likely the model has made a reply than the reference did. The loss is small when the preferred reply gained more than the rejected one.

Note the data: DPO learns from a fixed set of pairs written before training, so it is off-policy. An on-policy method such as the GRPO loop in online RL instead scores replies the current model has just produced.

Why the preference stays on the text channel. The duplex base model applies a length-normalised DPO (each reply's log-likelihood divided by its length, so long replies do not win by having more tokens) to text-token probabilities only. Audio-token probabilities proved unstable, and the text stream still steers the voice because the audio is conditioned on it. Another recipe gives the reason: one preference label per reply is sparse, while speech generation is dense (thousands of audio tokens), and the two conflict when they share parameters. It limits preference updates to the semantic channel, anchors the acoustic behaviour with supervised training, and rebalances the two signals from statistics of the model's own rollouts, since a batch with little preference signal gives unreliable gradients. DPO on audio tokens is the riskier path; a speech-model Talker can still be tuned this way when pairs are ranked by objective measures such as recognition error and pause errors.

Preference data. One duplex alignment set had about 93,000 pairs, 27% about timing and 73% about content, labelled by a strong LLM judge. Content pairs can be judged from transcripts. Timing pairs need timestamps, which is why a text-only judge is weak there. Inference

Distillation. Another recipe distils a large text teacher into the speech model in two phases, with the goal of raising the speech mode to the level of the text mode. Off-policy first: the student trains on outputs the teacher already wrote, like copying a worked solution. Then on-policy: the student writes its own answers and training pulls its next-token probabilities toward the teacher's, again measured by KL. The general teacher-student idea is in Flow levers.

A reward-model RL stage follows in several recipes, covered next in online RL for speech generation. Metrics for judging the result are in Evaluation and problems.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Which tokens carry the loss Text tokens, audio tokens or both, with an acoustic anchor next to the preference loss Text is stable and steers the voice; audio carries delivery and is unstable; the anchor protects delivery but the balance needs tuning
Length normalisation Whether longer replies win by default Removes a length bias; adds a hyperparameter
Pair mix and judge Share of timing against content pairs, and who labels them Timing pairs fix turn behaviour, content pairs fix answers; an LLM judge is cheap and weak on timing, humans are better and slow
Ranking signal for the Talker Recognition error and pause error, instead of opinion Objective and cheap; blind to naturalness
Teacher size in distillation How strong the text target is A bigger teacher gives a higher ceiling and a larger mismatch with the student

What goes wrong

  • Audio-token instability. DPO on audio-token likelihoods can diverge. Restrict the loss to text tokens unless there is a reason not to.
  • Judge bias on timing. An LLM that reads a transcript cannot see a late interruption. Judge timing from timestamps or a dedicated model.
  • Teacher-student mismatch. The student cannot copy a teacher whose output it cannot represent; check the speech mode against the text mode.
  • Proxy metrics and silent regressions. Existing metrics are superficial proxies for conversation quality; one proposal scores semantic quality and interaction timing separately. Track safety and question-answering scores before and after preference tuning.
In the interview, say:

"I would do DPO on the text tokens, with length normalisation and a mix of content and timing pairs, because audio-token likelihoods are unstable. Timing needs timestamps, so I would not trust a text-only judge for it."
