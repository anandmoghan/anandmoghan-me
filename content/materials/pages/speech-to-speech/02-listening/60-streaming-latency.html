Measuring and tuning streaming latency

"How fast is this recogniser?" has no single answer. The delay setting tells you how long the model is built to wait, but a user feels something else: how long after a word was spoken it appears on screen.

Think of live subtitles. A viewer does not care that the subtitler works with a two-second buffer. They notice that each word shows up a beat late, and some words show up much later than others. Good streaming work therefore measures word by word, then trains the model against that measure.

How it works

Measure per word, not per model. The structural delay (a setting such as "wait 6 frames") is a weak proxy for the delay a user perceives. A word-level emission-delay metric (AWED) measures, for each word, how long after the word ends in the audio its text appears. The median over words is the headline number, and the tail shows the worst words.

Stop forcing the model to hold back. Training from fixed word alignments teaches the model to wait for the aligned time even when it already knows the word. Reinforcement learning removes that constraint. Group relative policy optimisation (GRPO) has the model sample several transcripts for the same audio, score each with a reward, and nudge the model (the policy) toward those that beat the group average; the method itself is in RL beyond TTS. With a reward that scores accuracy and delay together, a delayed-streams recogniser post-trained at a single 6-frame setting cut WER by 30.8% relative at an 80 ms structural delay and 5.7% at 480 ms, and lowered the median emission delay from 1.17 s to 1.04 s.

Penalise latency directly. Another route learns when to read more audio and when to write. A small policy network scores each incoming frame, and a minimum-latency loss penalises the expected delay against forced-alignment boundaries. On a Mandarin test the average latency fell by more than half with almost no change in error (figures in Streaming ASR families).

Make waiting cheap. Most steps in a delayed-stream model emit nothing. The factorized variant predicts "wait" with its own yes/no probability and runs the vocabulary softmax (the step that scores every possible token) only when the answer is "do not wait". That skipped the softmax on 66.89% of LibriSpeech steps, cut memory for a batch of 32 from 65.82 to 24.51 GiB, and gave a slightly lower WER at 480 ms (6.17 against 6.52).

End of turn inside the recogniser

A voice agent also needs to know when the user has finished. A silence timer is the usual answer, but it fails when a pause inside a sentence is longer than the gap between turns. Detectors, the audio front end and echo cancellation are covered in Turn-taking, backchannels and barge-in.

One recipe puts the decision inside the recogniser. A LoRA adapter on a 0.6B recogniser learns to transcribe and to predict end of turn in the same pass. It reached 0.97 boundary recall (the share of real turn ends it caught) at a median latency of 0.39 s, with 0.3 false fires per minute of speech. The other route is a duplex dialogue model whose ASR head sits beside its reply head, so the user's words come out of the same decoding pass that decides whether to speak.

The lesson of the first recipe is about labels. Every label used in training must be computable from the audio heard so far, because the deployed model never sees the future. Offline corpora often encode future audio in their labels, so a model can look excellent on them and fail live. The authors report a case where appending one second of silence moved a model's end-of-turn recall from 0.10 to 1.00, which shows how much the evaluation setup decides the result.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Per-word delay metric What you optimise and report: the median and tail of word emission delay Needs word timestamps on the test audio, which costs an alignment step.
Latency-aware reward (GRPO) Lets the model emit words earlier than the aligned time Post-training stage with sampling cost; the reward weights decide the balance of accuracy against delay.
Learned read/write policy When to consume more audio against when to output One more network to train; a loss weight sets the latency pressure.
Factorized wait Skips the vocabulary softmax on waiting steps A modest accuracy gain plus much lower memory; the prediction heads change, so existing checkpoints do not carry over. Inference
End-of-turn head Turn decisions from the recogniser, not a silence timer Labels must be causal. False fires interrupt the user; late fires add delay.

What goes wrong

  • Reporting the setting as the latency. A model set to a given delay can still show some words much later than that; quote the measured delay, and its tail, because a good median can coexist with a few very late words. Inference
  • Optimising speed alone. Training for earlier words without an accuracy term degrades the transcript; the reward must carry both. Inference
  • Labels that peek ahead. End-of-turn or word-boundary labels built from the full recording leak the future, and the model collapses in live use.

In the interview, say:

The delay setting is not the latency users feel, so I measure emission delay word by word and train against it: a reward on both accuracy and delay lets the model write words as soon as it knows them. For turn-taking I would put end-of-turn detection in the recogniser with strictly causal labels, instead of a silence timer.
