Streaming ASR families

Every streaming recogniser answers two questions. What sets the latency floor, and who decides when a word is final? The answers sort the field into a handful of families.

Think of captioning a live broadcast. You can caption in fixed batches, decide after every beat whether to write something, trail the speaker by a fixed delay, or publish only the words your last two drafts agree on. Each habit is a family. A newer habit skips the writing step: a model that labels each beat directly and never composes sentences at all.

The first three rows are the layouts from How audio meets text seen through latency. A chunked model reads audio in fixed chunks and emits each chunk's text followed by EOC, trained with CTC forced alignment and an auxiliary CTC loss weighted 0.5, so chunk length is the dial. A wait-token model predicts a blank after every input embedding, so the LLM decides per frame whether to speak; its latency is the 240 ms frame plus 960 ms of encoder right context. A transducer keeps its streaming alignment and a Llama replaces the prediction network.

Delayed streams put text and audio on a shared clock and simply delay the text. Because the text trails by d, the model has d of audio to lean on when it writes each word, and ASR is the same model as TTS with the delay reversed. Voxtral Realtime trains the delay as an input, so one model runs at 480 ms or 960 ms by choice. The same layout works for the user's side of a duplex dialogue model: a lightweight ASR head next to the reply head reached 10.21 average WER on the Open ASR set inside a full-duplex model, and 7.73 trained as a standalone streaming model.

Learned read/write replaces the fixed delay with a small policy network that decides per frame whether to read more audio or let the LLM write, with a latency loss that pushes it to read as little as it can get away with. Wrappers put a policy around an offline model instead: LocalAgreement takes the longest common prefix of two successive updates over a buffer of about 30 s trimmed at sentence boundaries, and Simul-Whisper stops decoding at the end of the received audio using cross-attention.

Two families drop the LLM. A cache-aware FastConformer with an RNN-T head exposes the chunk size as its latency dial. A CTC-only model has no text decoder: each frame yields a label or blank, so there is nothing to wait for except the encoder itself, and greedy decoding (take the single best label at each frame) is one pass. IBM's TurboCTC is a 470M-parameter encoder that takes 100 Hz features down to 12.5 Hz; the authors call it twice as fast as the fastest competitor. The sources I read do not describe a streaming mode for it, so treat it as the speed ceiling that LLM-based recognisers are compared against. Inference

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Chunk length or segment size Audio per emission (chunked models), or the transducer's processing unit 2.56 s to 0.32 s moves WER from 2.5 / 6.5 to 3.1 / 7.8. Transducer-Llama runs 160 ms segments.
Encoder right context Future audio before a frame is used Speech ReaLLM pays 960 ms; more context is more accuracy and more delay. See look-ahead.
Text delay d How far the text trails the audio DSM's default is 2.5 s, with word-delay precision of about 300 ms. A shorter delay is faster and gives the model less audio to lean on. The setting is only a proxy for the delay users see: see Measuring and tuning streaming latency, which also covers latency-aware post-training and the cheaper waiting step.
Tokens per step Tokens the decoder proposes at once (ParaASR: 6, with 5.0 accepted on average) Fewer passes. Speed is quoted as real-time factor (RTF): processing time divided by audio duration, so 0.0053 means one second of audio takes about 5 ms. ParaASR reached 0.0053 on 30 s clips, against 0.0094 for Qwen3-ASR-1.7B in the same test. The inverse, RTFx, counts how many times faster than real time. The extra prediction branches must be trained.
Decoder-free output CTC-only recognisers drop the text decoder Very high RTFx, but no LLM in the loop, so no language-model knowledge at decode time. Inference
Agreement rule How many updates must agree before commit (n = 2 in LocalAgreement) Higher agreement is more stable and slower. Inference

Inference If the agent needs partials at a fixed, acceptable delay, a delayed stream is the cleanest, and a trained-in delay knob lets one model serve several budgets. If you already run Whisper, Simul-Whisper needs no retraining and LocalAgreement is the simplest, at more WER for the same latency.

What goes wrong

  • Partial instability. Wrappers re-run the model on a growing buffer, so earlier words can change. Committing only the agreed prefix is the defence, at the price of delay. Chunk-boundary cuts, padding and silence hallucination are covered in Streaming encoders.
  • Reading the setting as the latency. A delay or chunk setting is a design parameter; the delay a user sees is measured per word. Inference
  • Incomparable numbers. The figures above come from different hardware and test sets, and some sources contradict themselves (Simul-Whisper's abstract and table disagree). Compare shapes, not digits.

In the interview, say:

Streaming ASR comes in a few families: chunks with an end-of-chunk token, a per-frame wait token, a transducer with an LLM predictor, a delayed text stream, or a read/write policy on top of Whisper. Chunk length or delay is the one dial that trades WER for latency, and delayed text streams make ASR a special case of a multistream LM. I would measure latency per word, not by the setting, and a CTC-only model is the speed baseline to beat.
