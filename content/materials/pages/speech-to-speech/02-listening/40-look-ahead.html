Look-ahead and right context

To decide what a sound was, it often helps to hear what follows it. Look-ahead, also called right context, is how much future audio the encoder may see before it commits to the output for the current frame.

Zero look-ahead means each output depends only on the past, so it leaves as soon as its frame arrives. N frames of look-ahead means each output waits for N more frames. That helps resolve ambiguous sounds and word boundaries, and it adds latency. Look-ahead is the dial between a fast guess and a settled answer.

How it works

Configurations are often named by look-ahead in encoder frames, such as "0LA" or "12LA". Inference What 12 frames costs depends on the frame duration after downsampling: 480 ms at 40 ms per frame (4x subsampling of a 10 ms hop) and 960 ms at 80 ms per frame (8x). It also depends on how look-ahead is built. In regular look-ahead every self-attention layer peeks M frames ahead, and the reach compounds with depth. In chunk-aware look-ahead the input is cut into chunks of C frames; each frame sees its whole chunk plus earlier chunks held in the KV cache from Streaming encoders, but never the next chunk.

Regular Chunk-aware
Effective look-ahead M frames times the number of layers From C - 1 frames (first in chunk) down to 0 (last)
Example from the paper 17 layers, one frame each, 80 ms per frame: 17 × 80 ms = 1,360 ms Average latency is half the look-ahead size
RNN-T word error rate at 1,360 ms 7.1% 6.3%

So the same label is cheap or expensive depending on whether it means 12 frames once per chunk or 12 frames per layer.

The latency and accuracy curve below is measured on LibriSpeech test-other with a hybrid model and chunk-aware look-ahead. A hybrid model shares one encoder between two heads: an RNN-T head (the transducer from How audio meets text) and a CTC head. The table reports both as word error rate (WER, lower is better).

Most of the gain arrives within a few hundred milliseconds. Zero look-ahead gives a WER about 50% higher than 1,360 ms (9.5 against 6.3, RNN-T): about one word in ten is wrong, against about one in sixteen. For contrast, buffered streaming without a cache reaches only 11.3% WER at 2,000 ms, worse than cache-aware streaming with zero look-ahead.

Look-ahead in encoders built to stream

The cache-aware FastConformer retrofits look-ahead onto a standard design. Newer encoders put the dial in the architecture or the training recipe.

Model Where the look-ahead sits Reported setting and accuracy
Moonshine v2 Sliding window of 16 frames of left context; 4 frames of right context in the first two and last two layers, none in the middle ones (20 ms frames) 80 ms look-ahead. The medium model averages 6.65 WER on the Open ASR set.
Voxtral Realtime A chosen delay between audio and text, trained over 80 to 2400 ms and fed to the decoder LibriSpeech WER at 480 ms and 960 ms (figures in Streaming encoders)
Nemotron Speech streaming Cache-aware encoder with a right-context setting measured in 80 ms frames Chunks of 0.08, 0.16, 0.56 and 1.12 s give average WER 8.43, 7.67, 7.07 and 6.93

The curve has the same shape everywhere: the first few hundred milliseconds buy most of the accuracy, and the last second buys little. What changed is that a single model can now be run at any point on the curve.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Look-ahead amount Future frames visible per output More look-ahead lowers WER (9.5 down to 6.3 across the table) and adds latency.
Regular or chunk-aware Whether reach compounds with depth Chunk-aware gave 6.3% against 7.1% at the same 1,360 ms. It needs chunked training and a cache.
Which layers see ahead Right context only in some layers (Moonshine v2: the first two and last two) Keeps the total look-ahead small, but the placement must be tuned. Inference
Multi-look-ahead or trained-in delay One model trained on several settings, or conditioned on a delay (Voxtral Realtime) Picks latency at inference time at the cost of a wider training range. RNN-T WER at 40, 240 and 520 ms was 6.2, 5.5 and 5.2 for the multi model against 6.4, 5.9 and 5.4 for single models.
Frame duration Milliseconds per frame, set by downsampling Coarser frames make each look-ahead frame costlier in time (12 frames: 480 ms against 960 ms).

Inference Zero look-ahead suits barge-in detection, end-of-turn signals and the fastest partials, where reacting early matters more than a perfect word. A larger setting suits final transcripts handed to the reasoning LLM, and a multi-look-ahead encoder serves both from one model. NVIDIA lists a cache-aware streaming FastConformer-RNN-T with 80 to 160 ms latency for end-of-utterance detection; see End of turn inside the recogniser.

What goes wrong

  • Compounding look-ahead. One frame per layer across 17 layers at 80 ms per frame is 1,360 ms, far more than the 80 ms the setting looks like.
  • Quoting the wrong latency. Chunk-aware look-ahead varies across a chunk, so the average is half the maximum. State which one you mean.
  • Too little right context. Word boundaries and ambiguous sounds are the first to suffer.
  • Cache-free streaming. Recomputing overlapping buffers is both slower and less accurate than a cache-aware encoder at the same latency, as the 11.3% against 9.5% comparison shows.

In the interview, say:

Look-ahead is the future audio the encoder waits for before committing a frame, so it trades latency for accuracy. In NVIDIA's cache-aware FastConformer, RNN-T WER goes from 9.5% at zero look-ahead to 6.3% at 1,360 ms, and chunk-aware look-ahead beats per-layer look-ahead at the same latency because it does not compound with depth. I would train one encoder with several look-aheads, using zero for turn-taking and a larger one for final transcripts.
