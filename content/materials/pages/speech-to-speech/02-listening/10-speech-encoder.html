What a speech encoder does

A speech LLM cannot read a waveform. One second of 16 kHz audio is 16,000 numbers, and almost none of them say anything about words. The speech encoder is the ear: it turns audio into a short sequence of vectors, one per small slice of time, that capture what is said and how.

Think of a stenographer. They do not write down air pressure; they keep a compact record that preserves the content. The encoder makes that record, and an adapter after it shortens the record again until the LLM can afford to read it.

How it works

Four stages turn audio into LLM input, and each one shortens the sequence.

  1. Log-mel features. The audio is cut into overlapping 25 ms windows, one starting every 10 ms, and each window becomes a list of 80 numbers describing how much energy sits at each pitch band, on a scale that follows human hearing. The result is 100 frames per second. The construction is explained in Mel spectrograms.
  2. Convolutional stem. A convolution slides one small filter along the frames, like a short magnifying glass that reads three neighbouring frames and writes one summary per position. Stride is the step size of the slide; a stride of 2 moves two frames at a time, so the rate halves to one vector per 20 ms (50 Hz).
  3. Transformer encoder. Self-attention lets each frame look at the other frames and mix in the ones that help, so each output vector carries context and not just its own 20 ms. The vowel in "pen" against "pin" is hard to judge alone, but the neighbouring consonants and the rest of the word make it clear.
  4. Adapter (projector). The vectors are pooled or strided down again, typically to 25, 12.5 or 10 Hz, and multiplied by a learned matrix so each has exactly as many numbers as one LLM token embedding. That lets the LLM treat an audio vector like a word.

Shortening matters because the LLM pays for every position. The rate is the inverse of the frame duration, and the lengths follow directly.

\[ \text{rate (Hz)} = \frac{1000}{\text{ms per frame}}, \qquad 1800\,\text{s} \times 50\,\text{Hz} = 90{,}000, \qquad 1800\,\text{s} \times 12.5\,\text{Hz} = 22{,}500 \]

So thirty minutes of audio is 22,500 positions at 12.5 Hz; the comparison with 50 Hz and the frame rates are developed in Frames, hops and Hz. Most input paths pair an encoder pretrained on automatic speech recognition (ASR, speech to text) with a small adapter and a rate near 12.5 Hz. The rate is a design choice and not a law: Qwen3.5-Omni trained its audio encoder from scratch and runs it at 6.25 Hz, one vector per 160 ms. Discrete tokens appear on the input side only when the same vocabulary must also be generated; see Tokenizers and codecs.

The Whisper recipe, as the canonical example

Whisper is the encoder many speech LLMs start from, and its recipe is the template newer encoders copy or depart from. It is an encoder-decoder Transformer trained on 680,000 hours of weakly supervised audio, with task tokens (language, transcribe or translate, timestamps) that steer one decoder across ASR, translation and language ID. Its encoder takes a fixed 30 s window: 3,000 log-mel frames, halved by the stem to 1,500, with short audio padded up.

Speech LLMs keep the encoder, drop the decoder and add an adapter; Voxtral, for example, uses a 4x adapter to bring 50 Hz down to 12.5 Hz. The newest streaming systems no longer start from Whisper at all. Voxtral Realtime trains a causal encoder from scratch (50 Hz, sliding attention window of 15 s) and then applies the same 4x adapter. Streaming encoders covers how these work.

Reading the error numbers in this chapter

Word error rate (WER) counts the words a transcript gets wrong (substitutions, deletions and insertions) divided by the number of words in the reference. Lower is better. If the reference is "the cat sat on the mat" (6 words) and the model writes "the cat sat on mat today", one word is deleted and one inserted, so WER is 2 / 6, about 33%. Clean read speech scores a few percent; noisy or conversational speech scores higher.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Downsampling rate Positions per second the LLM reads (10 to 25 Hz is the common range; 6.25 Hz at the low end) Fewer positions and a cheaper LLM, but less fine acoustic detail. Some systems keep a 50 Hz continuous path next to 12.5 Hz tokens to win detail back.
Adapter type How frames merge: strided MLP (Voxtral), stride-2 pooling (Qwen2-Audio), windowed Q-Former (Granite-speech) Stride and pooling keep temporal order and are simple. A Q-Former (a small set of learned queries that pick what to keep from the frames) adapts to the content, at the cost of another component to train.
Encoder pretraining ASR-pretrained encoder against one trained from scratch ASR pretraining gives text-aligned features cheaply. Qwen3.5-Omni and Voxtral Realtime trained their own for a streaming-friendly design; each costs a full training run.
Frozen LLM Canary-Qwen trains encoder, projection and LoRA with the LLM frozen. LoRA adds small trainable low-rank matrices beside the frozen weights, so only a tiny share of the model is updated. Guards against forgetting text skills, which training the LLM along with the encoder can erode.

What goes wrong

  • Padding cost. A two-second turn still costs a 30 s encoder pass when the window is fixed, and a fixed window with bidirectional attention rules out live use as shipped.
  • Hallucination on silence. The Whisper model card warns that predictions may include text that was not spoken, and that the model is prone to repetition. A study that masked parts of the audio found that most of Whisper-small's inserted words were repetition loops, while decoders built on an explicit LLM inserted far fewer. Teams add voice activity detection (VAD, a small model that marks which stretches contain speech) before the model and measure hallucination on their own silence and noise clips.

The encoder turns 100 Hz log-mel frames into vectors, and an adapter pools them to about 12.5 Hz so the LLM sees a short sequence. Whisper's recipe is the template (large weakly supervised data, a convolutional stem, a Transformer), but its fixed 30 s bidirectional window is wrong for live use, so the newest streaming models train a causal encoder from scratch.
