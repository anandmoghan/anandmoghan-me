The conversation clock

Picture a metronome with two musicians. On every tick one may play and the other may rest, but the tick never stops. A duplex voice model (one that listens and speaks at the same time) works the same way. It advances in fixed steps, and at each step it takes in a slice of the user's audio and puts out a slice of its own.

That step is the frame, as in Frames, hops and Hz, and about 12.5 frames per second (one every 80 ms) is the common choice. Silence is not a special state. It is a frame whose text is padding and whose audio is quiet. So a model on this clock needs no explicit "whose turn is it" variable.

How it works

One shared clock. User audio, agent audio and agent text all share one frame index. Frame \( t \) of the user stream and frame \( t \) of the agent stream describe the same 80 ms of real time. The model reads the user's frame, then writes the agent's frame, then moves on.

What is emitted per frame. The audio is coded as in Codec mechanics: eight codebook entries per frame, the first for coarse content and each later one for finer detail. In the canonical dual-stream model, each frame therefore carries 1 text slot, 8 agent audio codes and 8 user audio codes: \( 1 + 8 + 8 = 17 \) sub-tokens. A big transformer runs once per frame over time. A small one then expands its output into the 17 sub-tokens, text first, the split described in Multi-codebook patterns.

The per-frame deadline. Real-time audio must keep flowing, so the work for frame \( t \) has to finish within 80 ms. A late frame is not slower output; it is an audible gap. The real-time factor from Audio meets text is the usual check:

\[ \text{RTF} = \frac{t_{\text{compute}}}{t_{\text{audio}}} \]

An RTF below 1 is necessary but not enough. The worst single frame must still fit inside its slot.

The minimum reaction quantum. The model can only change behaviour at a frame boundary, or at a chunk boundary if it works in chunks. A decision made at a random moment waits half a chunk on average:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{wait}] = \frac{c}{2} \]

That is 40 ms for an 80 ms frame, 120 ms for a 240 ms chunk and 500 ms for a 1 s chunk. The quantum sets a floor on how fast the model can start talking, stop talking or say "mm-hm".

Why text leads speech. Text is the first sub-step of each frame, so that frame's audio is conditioned on it. Text is also sparse: about 65% of text slots in English conversation are padding, so a word costs one slot and its speech takes several frames. The model can therefore commit to the words slightly before it voices them. Inference That lead is the room a model has to plan a sentence while still speaking the previous one. Producing text and speech at the same time covers how the lead is set.

Where the latency comes from. The reference model's theoretical latency is 160 ms and about 200 ms in practice. The 160 ms is one 80 ms frame plus one acoustic-delay step of 80 ms, the lag by which the finer audio codes trail the first. Model-by-model detail: Rates, frames and clocks.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Frame rate Steps per second, so deadline length and sequence length 12.5 Hz keeps long recordings short (the 30-minute arithmetic is in Frames, hops and Hz); a lower rate also makes decisions coarser
Codebooks per frame Sub-steps inside the deadline and audio fidelity More codebooks sound better but every one is another serial step before the slot closes
Big and small transformer split Which model runs 12.5 times a second and which runs 17 times a step Cheap expansion keeps the deadline, but the small model sees only a summary vector
Chunk size How often the backbone wakes and how long a decision waits Bigger chunks save compute; they add the \( c/2 \) wait and slower reactions
Acoustic delay How many frames the later codebooks trail the first Less delay lowers latency; the base model used more delay in pretraining and less in fine-tuning
Text-audio delay How far text leads its speech A longer lead gives more planning room and adds latency before the voice starts

The trade-off column is engineering reasoning, not a measured ablation, apart from the sequence-length arithmetic. The practical rule: pick the slowest frame rate whose decisions still feel immediate, then spend the saved budget on a faster big-model pass.

What goes wrong

  • Missed deadlines. A single slow frame is an audible gap. Teams measure the worst frame and the 95th and 99th percentile, not only the mean.
  • Mixed-up clocks. "12.5 Hz" is the frame rate; the token rate is 100 per second with eight codebooks. Quote sample rate, frame rate, token rate and chunk size separately.
  • Quantised decisions. A model that decides once per chunk reacts late and feels stiff, even if the speech is clean.
  • Theory against practice. A theoretical 160 ms and a measured 200 ms are different quantities; compare like with like.
In the interview, say:

"A duplex model runs on one frame clock, 80 ms at 12.5 Hz, and every frame it reads the user and writes text and audio, silence included. So I budget the worst frame, not the mean, and I remember that decisions wait half a chunk on average. Text leads speech by design, which is where planning room comes from."
