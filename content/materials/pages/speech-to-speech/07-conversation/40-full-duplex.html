Full duplex and the turn-taking feel

A walkie-talkie is half duplex: one side talks, the other waits. A phone call is full duplex: both sides can speak and hear at the same moment. A full-duplex voice model listens while it speaks, so overlap, backchannels and interruptions come from the model's own decisions and not from a silence timer.

The design question is where the speak-or-listen decision lives. There are six common answers, and the choice explains most of what a duplex model feels like to talk to.

How it works

Design Where the decision lives Canonical example
Dual-stream Implicit: both audio streams are tokens on every frame, and silence is tokens too Moshi, on one 80 ms clock
Flattened The order of chunks: input speech, output text and output speech in one sequence OmniFlatten, with text chunks of 2 and speech chunks of 10
Control head A tiny add-on reads the LLM's internal summary of the audio so far (its hidden states) and acts as a gate, choosing "keep listening" or "speak" Freeze-Omni keeps the LLM frozen and predicts a chunk-wise state
Time-division Chunks of input and output alternate on a coarse clock SyncLLM with 160 to 240 ms chunks
Action channel An explicit token each chunk: respond, interrupt or backchannel DuplexSLA, on a 160 ms chunk clock
Token-state A few special tokens added to an ordinary LLM vocabulary; the LLM itself says when to listen, speak or stop A model fine-tuned from an existing speech LLM with only special tokens added

The token-state route is the cheapest to adopt: no new module, only a few extra tokens, so it reuses the existing training and serving stack. Its authors report 92% turn-taking success and 100% interruption success on one benchmark after fine-tuning on a few hundred thousand duplex samples, and a better speech-response score than Moshi on the same test. Those numbers come from their own benchmark.

A 2026 survey frames the designs as levels. L0 is an external scheduler around a half-duplex model, L1 is a control head, and L2 encodes the duties in the token stream itself. Its line to remember: architecture sets the capacity, training realises it.

What causes the turn-taking feel. Two causes follow from the sources, and both are about the decision, not the voice quality. Inference

  1. Decisions quantised at chunk boundaries. As in The conversation clock, a decision waits \( c/2 \) on average for chunk length \( c \). Systems that alternate user and assistant chunks only switch at boundaries, which a survey calls "apparent (not substantive)" full duplex.
  2. Scarce interaction data. Real two-channel conversation with overlap is rare, so models learn timing from a small amount of it, or from synthetic dialogue with regular timing.

A related finding is that the bottleneck is not the speech path. It is the duplex decision, plus ingesting the user's audio while the model speaks its own. Inference The behaviours the decision must produce are the ones in Turn-taking, backchannels and barge-in.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Decision granularity How often the model can switch between listening and speaking Per frame is most natural and most expensive; chunk-wise saves compute and quantises the decision
Action channel Whether the decision is an explicit, supervised output Measurable and trainable; needs labels for each action
Frozen LLM plus head How much text ability survives Keeps intelligence by construction; the head sees only hidden states
Look-ahead Future frames used before committing One block of look-ahead costs one block of delay
Predicting several frames per wake How often the backbone must run Reduces compute per second; hard to keep timing sharp
Steering after training Whether tone, persona, speaking rate and voice style follow instructions A Moshi-based model fine-tuned for steering improves its audio-steering pass rate over the open baselines it was tested against; a later reinforcement-learning stage improved interruption response but exposed reward hacking, where the model earned reward by giving incomplete answers
Interaction data mix What overlap patterns the model sees Real two-channel speech teaches timing; synthetic dialogue scales and carries artefacts

A choice for a product that must reason and talk together: delay streams plus an action channel, or a token-level L2 design. What goes wrong

  • Latency numbers that do not compare. Theoretical, first-packet and turn-taking delays are different quantities on different hardware. Quote a number with its definition, never as a ranking.
  • Synthetic-data timing artefacts. Dialogue written by an LLM and rendered by TTS has regular timing and inserted backchannels at fixed spots. The model copies the regularity. Mix in real two-channel conversation and compare backchannel timing with the human distribution.
  • Instruction gaps. One benchmark finds the best of six full-duplex systems follows turn-taking instructions in under two thirds of cases.
  • A demo is not an evaluation. Ask for false-yield rate, stop latency and multi-turn results, not a recorded conversation. The tool-use benchmark in Using tools while talking shows why: the accuracy leader was not the fastest, and the fastest did not reliably take its turn.
In the interview, say:

"The hard part of duplex is the decision, not the voice. It is quantised by chunk size and shaped by the interaction patterns in the training data. So I would compare duplex systems on interaction metrics at one operating point, not on a single latency number."
