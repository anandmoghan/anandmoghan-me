Waveform and sample rate

Sound is air pressure changing over time. A microphone measures that pressure many times per second and stores the readings as a list of numbers. That list is the waveform, and the number of readings per second is the sample rate.

Think of a flip-book: more pages per second capture faster motion. A higher sample rate captures higher frequencies (higher pitches and the hiss of consonants), at the price of more numbers to store, move and generate. The rest of this chapter is about shrinking those numbers into something a model can read, one short slice of audio (a frame) at a time.

How it works

Each sample is one pressure reading, so a 16 kHz recording holds 16,000 numbers per second. The time between samples is the inverse of the rate:

\[ T_s = \frac{1}{f_s}, \qquad f_s = 16000\ \text{Hz} \;\Rightarrow\; T_s = 62.5\ \mu\text{s} \]

A sampled signal cannot represent frequencies above half the sample rate (the Nyquist limit). Anything above it is either removed before sampling or folds back as false lower frequencies, which is called aliasing. So the sample rate sets the bandwidth of the audio, and speech systems use a handful of standard rates:

Sample rate Where it appears Band limit (half the rate)
8 kHz Telephone audio, such as the Fisher corpus 4 kHz
16 kHz Typical ASR input, including Whisper 8 kHz
24 kHz Common for generation, such as the Mimi codec 12 kHz
44.1 kHz Full-band codecs such as DAC 22.05 kHz

The cost of bandwidth is length. At 24 kHz, a model that predicted samples directly would take 24,000 steps per second. A codec running at 12.5 frames per second covers 1,920 samples in each frame, so a model that works on frames sees a sequence 1,920 times shorter. That is why speech models predict tokens or latents and leave sample-level detail to a decoder (tokens to waveform).

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Sample rate (16 vs 24 vs 44.1 kHz) Bandwidth: how much high-frequency detail (sibilants, breath, air) survives More samples to decode and store; needs training data that really has that bandwidth
Match input rate to the front end ASR front ends expect 16 kHz; generation stacks often run at 24 kHz Resampling between stages is cheap, but each stage fixes its own bandwidth limit
Upsample low-rate data (8 to 24 kHz) Lets telephone data train a 24 kHz model; Moshi upsamples Fisher with a diffusion super-resolution model The generator invents plausible high frequencies; it does not recover them
Predict frames, not samples Sequence length for the language model Needs a decoder to turn frames back into samples

What goes wrong

  • Nominal rate, real bandwidth. A file labelled 16 kHz can hold audio that was recorded at 8 kHz and upsampled. The DAC authors warn that source rates vary (Common Voice is commonly 8 to 16 kHz), and that models trained on such data often fail above the data's true rate. Check the spectrogram, not the header.
  • High rate is not high quality. Stable Audio Open generates 44.1 kHz stereo, yet its authors state it cannot produce intelligible speech or singing.
  • Aliasing from naive resampling. Dropping samples without low-pass filtering folds high frequencies into the audible band as buzz.
In the interview, say:

"The sample rate sets the bandwidth: 16 kHz for ASR, 24 kHz for most speech generation, 44.1 kHz for full-band codecs. Models never predict samples directly; at 24 kHz that is 24,000 steps a second. They predict frames or tokens and let a decoder fill in the samples."
