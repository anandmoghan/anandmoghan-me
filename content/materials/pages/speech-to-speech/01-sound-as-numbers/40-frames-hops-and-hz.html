Frames, hops and Hz

A speech system runs several clocks at once, and "the rate" can mean any of them. Samples arrive thousands of times a second. Spectrogram frames (the slices from the previous two sections) arrive 100 times a second. The language model sees a few dozen steps a second. Keep the clocks apart and every number in a speech paper becomes easy to check. One rule does most of the work:

\[ \text{rate (Hz)} = \frac{1000}{\text{ms per frame}} \]

So 10 ms per frame is 100 Hz, 20 ms is 50 Hz, 40 ms is 25 Hz, and 80 ms is 12.5 Hz.

How it works: the rate ladder

Each stage between the microphone and the model divides the rate by a fixed factor. Input audio goes down this ladder, because each language-model position is expensive; generated audio goes back up it.

Stage Rate Period Worked arithmetic
Waveform 16 kHz (ASR), 24 kHz (Mimi) 62.5 \( \mu\text{s} \) at 16 kHz \( 1/16000 \)
Mel frames 100 Hz 10 ms hop 25 ms window and 10 ms hop at 16 kHz are 400 and 160 samples
Whisper encoder 50 Hz 20 ms Stride-2 conv: \( 100/2 = 50 \). A 30 s chunk is 1,500 frames
Adapter into the LLM 12.5 Hz 80 ms Voxtral's 4x adapter: \( 50/4 = 12.5 \)
Codec frame (Mimi) 12.5 Hz 80 ms \( 24000/12.5 = 1920 \) samples per frame

Other encoders pool to different rates, but the pattern is the same: read fast, pool before the language model. The encoder side is covered in Listening.

Frames, tokens and bitrate

On the output side, a frame becomes discrete integer ids (tokens) through a codebook, which is a lookup table of learned sound pieces. A codec that uses several codebooks per frame emits several tokens per frame, so token rate and frame rate differ. Mimi runs at 12.5 Hz with 8 codebooks, which is \( 12.5 \times 8 = 100 \) tokens per second. The bitrate formula is in Why tokenize and the full codebook mechanics are in Codec mechanics; how these clocks line up with text and the second speaker is in Conversation.

Why about 12.5 Hz is common

Three reasons stack up. First, sequence length: thirty minutes is 1,800 s, which is 90,000 positions at 50 Hz but 22,500 at 12.5 Hz. Second, text matching: with English text at roughly 3 to 4 tokens a second, 12.5 Hz leaves about 3 frames per text token. Third, one tokenizer study reports word error rate (the share of words transcribed wrongly) of 23.0 at 6.25 Hz, 4.06 at 12.5 Hz and 3.05 at 25 Hz. Going from 12.5 to 25 Hz buys little; going down to 6.25 Hz costs a lot.

Levers and optimisations

The sample rate, window, hop and mel-bin levers are in the previous three sections. The ones specific to the rate ladder are:

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Encoder pooling or stride Rate into the LLM (50 Hz to 12.5 Hz with a 4x adapter) Discards fine acoustic detail
Codec frame rate Output steps per second of audio, and reaction granularity (one frame is the smallest unit a model can emit) Lower is cheaper for the LM; too low hurts intelligibility
Codebooks per frame Token rate and bitrate More codebooks improve reconstruction but lengthen the sequence

What goes wrong

Terms get mixed up.

"24 kHz" is a sample rate. "100 Hz" is a feature rate. "12.5 Hz" is a frame rate. "100 tokens per second" is a token rate, and it is 12.5 Hz times 8 codebooks. A chunk is a batch of frames, so it is a latency choice, not a rate.

  • Frame rate against token rate. The LM sees 100 tokens per second on the audio side alone for Mimi, and 200 with a user stream plus an agent stream.
  • Sample rate against frame rate. One codec can be both 24 kHz and 12.5 Hz: each frame then covers 1,920 samples.
  • Going too low. 6.25 Hz is viable only when extra information is available at decode time; one very low-rate codec needs the transcript.
  • Padding hides compute. Whisper pads short audio to 30 s, so cost is paid for the full 1,500 frames.
In the interview, say:

"I keep five numbers apart. Sample rate is samples per second, like 24 kHz. Feature rate is the 100 Hz mel hop, encoder rate is 50 Hz, and the rate into the LLM is pooled to 12.5 Hz. Token rate is frame rate times codebooks, 100 per second for Mimi."
