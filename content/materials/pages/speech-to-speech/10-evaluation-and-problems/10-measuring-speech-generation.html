Measuring speech generation

You cannot unit-test a voice. There is no exact answer to compare against, so every automatic score is a proxy: a second model listens to the output and reports one number. A recogniser checks that the words are right, a speaker model checks that it sounds like the target, and a rating predictor guesses how natural it sounds.

Think of a restaurant critic who tastes the dish through three machines: one for ingredients, one for resemblance to a reference dish, one for overall appeal. Each machine is cheap and repeatable, and each has a blind spot. The skill is knowing which blind spot each one has, and keeping a human taster for the final call.

How it works

Each metric pipes the generated audio through a fixed evaluator and scores the result. The table lists the usual five, what each one listens for, and where it fails.

Metric How it is computed What it misses
Intelligibility (WER) A fixed ASR model transcribes the output; word error rate (Listening) against the input text Prosody, voice and naturalness. A flat robotic read can score a perfect zero
Speaker similarity (SIM) Cosine similarity between speaker-verification embeddings (vectors that summarise who is speaking) of the reference and the output Style and emotion. The number depends on which embedding model you used
Predicted MOS A network trained on human ratings predicts a mean opinion score (MOS, as in Judging a tokenizer) Fidelity to a reference. Scores saturate near or above real recordings
Emotion similarity An emotion embedding model compares reference or target emotion with the output Whether the emotion fits the words
Human listening tests Panels rate naturalness (MOS), compare two systems side by side (CMOS) or rate resemblance to the reference (SMOS) Nothing structural, but slow, costly and noisy

The evaluator is part of the measurement, so each metric is only comparable when the evaluator is the same. A public benchmark such as Seed-TTS-eval fixes one recogniser per language (Whisper-large-v3 for English, Paraformer-zh for Mandarin) and one speaker model (a WavLM-large fine-tuned for verification). Inference A recogniser has its own language model, so it can guess a plausible word from a mumbled one and forgive errors a listener would hear. It also errs more on some accents and languages, so a rise in WER can mean the ASR is weak on that slice, not that the TTS got worse. Likewise, a SIM of 0.70 from one speaker model does not compare with 0.70 from another. A non-standard model is fine for tracking your own progress, not for a claim against a published number.

Predicted MOS has a ceiling. UTMOS, the common predictor, scores naturalness, not closeness to a reference. A flow-matching TTS can score above ground truth on one test set and below it on another (figures in High fidelity), and a codec scores 4.13 against 4.09 ground truth. When the system already matches real recordings, the predictor stops discriminating, and a human panel is the only way to see further gains. Synthesia, for example, reports a blind test with 100 native English-speaking evaluators across 17 accents, though it publishes no scores or comparison systems. CMOS and SMOS (the human comparison and resemblance ratings above) are the new pieces here; the codec-level metrics are in the tokenizer chapter.

Levers and optimisations

Lever What it changes Trade-off
Pin the evaluator versions (ASR, speaker model, MOS predictor) Makes scores comparable across runs and with published tables Locks you into that model's blind spots
Score with a second ASR and speaker model that training never saw Exposes reward hacking and evaluator over-fit Extra compute and a second set of versions to track
Report per slice (accent, language, entity type, utterance length) Shows where an average hides a failing group Needs labelled slices and larger test sets
Quote predicted MOS next to the ground-truth score Tells you whether the number can still discriminate Needs matched reference recordings
Resynthesis WER on every change, human panels as periodic gates Cheap regression catch with an occasional ground-truth check Panels are slow, so regressions can ship between gates

Reporting several metrics together (WER, SIM, emotion, predicted MOS) closes the loopholes any one score leaves open, and the same bundle works as a training reward. The caution is that anything you optimise stops being a trustworthy test, so keep a held-out judge for evaluation. A learned judge is itself a model to validate: a benchmark that turns MOS datasets into pairwise tests found models weaker on synthetic speech than on human speech and on clips with near-equal ratings, so check a judge on close pairs before trusting it. What goes wrong

  • Scores rise, listeners disagree. Optimising WER and SIM can produce monotone speech; one study found that RL on transcription rewards lowered error rates while prosody collapsed.
  • Single-predictor hacking. Optimising one naturalness predictor can exploit its quirks, so use an ensemble or a calibrated composite, and hold out judges and listeners.
  • Incomparable or unreported evaluators. Different ASR and speaker models make the same nominal metric incomparable across papers, and a score without the model name and version cannot be reproduced.
In the interview, say:

"WER, speaker similarity and predicted MOS are proxies, and each is only comparable with the same evaluator version. I would pin them, report them per accent and language, use a second held-out evaluator for gating, and keep human listening panels as the release gate."
