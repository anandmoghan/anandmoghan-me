Measuring conversation

A voice agent can have perfect words and a perfect voice and still feel broken, because it answers too late, cuts you off, or stops talking when you say "mm-hm". Conversation quality is mostly about timing, so the metrics are time differences between events.

Think of timing a relay race: the stopwatch starts at the starter's gun, not when the runner noticed it. In conversation the gun is the moment the human actually finished their turn, which the system itself only estimates. So every metric below starts its clock from a labelled ground-truth event, never from the system's own detector.

How it works

Annotators mark gold events on a dual-channel recording: the end of a user turn, the onset of a barge-in, a backchannel. The system's output is recorded on its own channel after echo cancellation (see Turn-taking), so its timestamps can be compared with the gold ones.

Metric Definition Reads as
Time to first audio Gold end of turn to the agent's first audio; report p50, p90, p95 Responsiveness
Floor-transfer offset Agent onset minus gold end of turn, as a distribution against human conversation Human timing; humans on smooth transfers start a median 151 ms before the other turn ends
Stop latency Gold barge-in onset to the last audible agent sample, plus the share of agent speech after onset How fast it yields
False-yield rate Stops caused by backchannels, laughter or background speech, per conversation minute Wrongly giving up the floor
Premature-takeover rate Share of user mid-turn pauses in which the agent starts speaking Wrongly grabbing the floor
Backchannel rate and placement Rate versus humans, and the share placed shortly after a prosodic boundary Whether "mm-hm" lands naturally

Latency means little on its own, because any detector can be made faster by being more trigger-happy. The fair way is to report it at a fixed error rate: mean latency at a 5% or 10% false-cutoff rate, or the false-cutoff rate at a fixed 300 ms or 600 ms budget. One vendor end-of-turn benchmark reports 9.9% false cutoffs at 300 ms but 4.5% at 600 ms for its own detector, which shows the curve you are choosing a point on. Vendor latency claims with no stated benchmark, such as a quoted end-of-turn time inside a speech-to-text API, cannot be compared with these.

Benchmarks come in three kinds. Scripted overlap suites such as Full-Duplex-Bench test pause handling, backchannels, turn-taking and interruption. Annotated dual-channel sets such as TurnBench give gold end-of-turn and interruption labels from three annotators. Judge-based sets use a trained turn-event model or an LLM to label behaviour at scale, and any such judge must first be checked against human labels on a subset before its scores gate a release.

Beyond timing: capability, instruction following and fairer scoring

Timing says when the agent speaks, not whether the answer was good. One group of benchmarks tests what the model knows and obeys; a newer group fixes how timing itself is scored.

Benchmark What it tests Worth knowing
VoiceBench Voice assistants given real and synthetic spoken instructions that vary by speaker, environment and content Built because earlier tests used clean speech and general knowledge only
MMAU Audio understanding and reasoning over speech, sound and music: 10,000 clips, 27 skills At release the best model scored 52.97%
URO-Bench Speech-to-speech dialogue on understanding, reasoning and oral conversation, in a basic and a pro track of 20 test sets each Open models trail their own backbone LLMs on instruction following
Intent-conditioned timing (TACT) Turn-taking scored against the speaker's likely intent, over 9,728 episodes (73.2 hours) Across 11 systems the best scores 0.47 against a human 0.86; agreement with human judgement is 0.81 against 0.46 for binary metrics
Implicit instruction following Whether an agent infers floor-management behaviour from a persona (case counts in Prompting speech LLMs) Obeying an explicit rule does not mean inferring it from a role

Many duplex benchmarks score a reply as right only inside a fixed window, so a delayed reply or an early overlap counts as a failure. The intent-conditioned approach argues the right timing depends on the speaker's latent intent, which can only be inferred from how they behave. It scores with a continuous ranked probability score, a proper score that gives partial credit, weighted by timing curves fitted to human floor-transfer offsets. Inference Report both the binary pass rate and the continuous score until you trust the new one.

Levers and optimisations

The levers are the system knobs these metrics expose. Each one moves two metrics in opposite directions, which is why metrics are reported in pairs.

Lever What it changes Trade-off
End-of-turn threshold or silence timeout Time to first audio versus premature takeover Faster replies cut users off mid-thought
Semantic end-of-turn model instead of silence Shifts the whole latency versus false-cutoff curve Does not remove the trade-off; adds a model to run
Barge-in rule (minimum duration or words, adaptive mode) False-yield rate versus stop latency Stricter rules ignore backchannels but yield later to real interruptions
Decision cadence Timing resolution of every event A decision made at chunk boundaries waits half a chunk on average (Conversation clock); smaller chunks cost compute
False-interruption recovery window Lets the agent resume if no real words follow a stop Resuming too eagerly talks over a real interruption
Intent-conditioned or continuous timing score Stops a valid delay or overlap counting as failure Closer to human judgement; needs intent annotations and a fitted human timing distribution

Where these decisions live in a full system, including echo cancellation and semantic end-of-turn models, is in Conversation, and how a streaming recogniser reports delay is in Listening. Model-by-model detail: the literature review.

What goes wrong

  • Starting the clock at the system's own detector. The detector (usually voice activity detection, VAD, as introduced in Listening) stops on silence, and that hides the time spent deciding the user was done, so latency looks better than it is.
  • Reporting the mean. A conversation is judged by its worst pauses, so report p95 and p99 under realistic concurrency.
  • Measuring stop latency without echo cancellation. The microphone can pick up the agent's own voice and distort both stop latency and false yields.
  • Reading self-reported or binary numbers as truth. Benchmark authors report their own systems under their own conditions, so quote them as claims, and a fixed pass-fail window punishes delayed replies that fit the speaker's intent.
  • Timing without content, or a demo as evidence. A model can match human timing and still answer poorly, and a recorded conversation shows neither false-yield rate nor multi-turn behaviour. The open problem, per the TurnBench authors, is that no current system matches human timing without excessive false positives.
In the interview, say:

"I would never report latency alone. I would report time to first audio from gold end of turn at a fixed false-yield rate, stop latency after barge-in on echo-cancelled output, and false yields per minute, anchored to the human offset. Judge-based metrics get validated on a human subset first."
