Common problems: audio-visual avatars

An avatar video is a relay: script to voice, voice to motion, motion to pixels. Each stage can add its own error, and later stages cannot always repair what earlier ones baked in. A mispronounced name reaches the lips as a correctly lip-synced wrong word.

Synthesia's Express-2 stack shows the shape: Express-Voice for speech, Express-Animate for motion from audio, Express-Eval for scoring motion candidates, and Express-Render for frames. Its Video Agents "talk, listen, and act in real time", so the duplex failures from the previous section reach the same product. What each level of interactive avatar (talk, listen, see) requires is in avatar platform design; most failures below are talking failures, and the listening ones get their own row.

Why sync metrics mislead. The standard lip-sync scores, Sync-C and Sync-D (also written LSE-C and LSE-D), come from SyncNet, a pretrained network that embeds a short stretch of mouth video and a short stretch of audio and checks whether they belong together. Sync-D is the distance between the two embeddings (lower is better); Sync-C is SyncNet's confidence that they match (higher is better). Neither needs a ground-truth video, which is why they are popular. Papers report that they correlate weakly with human judgement and give odd values, such as generated video beating the ground truth, and values from different papers use different protocols. Inference The practical response is phoneme-stratified human ratings, with the automatic score as a coarse alarm.

Why streaming errors compound. A live avatar uses a student trained to imitate a slow teacher in a few steps and to produce video one short chunk at a time, looking only backwards (distillation, from Diffusion and flow). Each chunk builds on the previous one, but in training the student saw clean teacher examples rather than its own slightly wrong output. A tiny flaw in chunk 10 is then input to chunk 11, so flaws snowball into flicker or black frames. The remedy is to train the student on its own rollouts so it learns to correct itself; "retrospective" distillation adds a step where the model looks back at what it has produced and repairs accumulated error. Inference The abstract of the retrospective method does not say how the looking back works.

Joint generation, a different route. A cascade makes the voice first and the face second, so the two can disagree. A joint model generates sound and picture together in one network, with each stream attending to the other, so lip movement and voice come out matched by construction. The cost is size and speed, so the 2026 pattern is to distil a joint teacher into a few-step streaming student, which inherits the coupling. For dubbing, one model adapted with a lightweight LoRA can write the translated voice and the matching mouth motion in one pass, instead of translating, retiming and re-rendering lips separately. Synthesia's own dubbing release claims a translation engine that respects the original timing and length, with no published metric.

What goes wrong

  • A published system with no published failure rates. The Express-2 post gives no quantitative results, failure cases or latency numbers, so which failures Synthesia measures is the best question to ask.
  • A listening avatar judged like a talking one. Lip-sync scores say nothing about nods and reactions, and a benchmark of real video calls found that many models ignore the video stream or fall back to generic captioning. Test listening behaviour separately.
  • Trusting a sync number, or fixing in the wrong stage. A high Sync-C score does not prove good lips on bilabials, and a mispronunciation cannot be fixed at the renderer; it belongs in the script or voice model.
  • Metric drift from re-encoding. Encoder settings change metric values, so score on one pinned pipeline, and compare similarity scores across systems only when the speaker encoder matches (Synthesia names WavLM).

Items 1 and 2 are cheap to test automatically and belong in every regression run; items 3 to 7 need human rating because the automatic metrics are weak; items 8 to 10 rank higher if dubbing, streaming or Video Agents is the focus.

In the interview, say:

"The Express-2 post publishes no failure metrics, so I would ask which ones you track. My first three would be per-entity pronunciation, an alignment-based hallucination rate, and phoneme-stratified lip-sync with human raters, because Sync-C correlates weakly with human judgement."
