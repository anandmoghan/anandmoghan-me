Real-time avatar and audio-visual platform design

An avatar is a voice with a face attached. The face is rendered from the speech, so the speech has to arrive first, on time, and carry enough information to drive expression. This section is about keeping voice and video on one timeline, and about running two very different products on one platform.

Think of a film dub: the soundtrack is the master clock and the picture is trimmed to fit. A live broadcast works the same way. If the network stalls, you drop video frames, never the audio.

Inference The design below is my opinion, built from sourced components. Latency numbers are illustrative targets, not measurements.

How it works

The platform serves two products with different service levels. Batch video turns a script into a 1080p, 30 fps video in minutes, and dubs it into other languages. Interactive Video Agents hold two-way conversations, where Synthesia describes real dialogue as "typically under one second". Both read the same identity and voice stores, and both sit behind a consent gate.

Talk, listen, see

Synthesia sorts interactive avatars into three levels, and the level sets what the renderer needs as input. The product question is which one to build first.

Level The avatar can Face is driven by What it needs beyond Level 1
1. Talk Speak, with lip sync and expression Its own audio only Nothing; this is where every current system on the market sits, per Synthesia
2. Listen Also react while the user speaks: nods, shifting expression, acknowledgements Its own audio and the user's audio The user's audio reaching the renderer; reactions generated alongside the avatar's own speech, which Synthesia describes as predicting audio and video jointly
3. See Also respond to posture, gesture and facial signals Audio plus the user's camera feed A camera stream and a vision encoder; falls back to Level 2 when the camera is off

Research shows what Levels 2 and 3 take. A head-avatar system takes in the user's audio and motion and answers spoken and nonverbal cues such as nods and laughter at about 500 ms, and a full-duplex avatar system reports 122 ms end to end by predicting a short continuation of the speech it expects. Measuring this is new: a benchmark of real video-call clips found that many models ignore the video stream and that a speech-then-avatar cascade cannot produce full-duplex nonverbal cues.

The renderer reads the voice. In the target design the renderer takes the agent's audio plus state from the speak head (speaking, yielding, listening) and optional viseme timing. Streaming generators come from distilling a bidirectional teacher into a causal few-step student, the recipe in Flow levers. Express-Render, Synthesia's renderer, outputs 1080p at 30 fps and is distilled to two diffusion steps; a streaming student is the natural extension. Inference

Better than a waveform is the voice model's token stream. Meta's research avatar sends the same speech tokens, which carry both what is said and how it is delivered, to the audio decoder and to the avatar model, so voice, lips and expression stay in step. The avatar is a diffusion transformer that generates video in short causal chunks, using the newest latents of each chunk as motion context for the next. Inference If the speak head already emits tokens, the renderer can read them plus state, so intent the LLM knew can reach the face. A fully joint audio-video model gives sync for free but is too slow for a call; one student is reported at about 99 times lower latency than its joint teacher. The trade is quality and voice control against sync and latency, so for a product whose voice identity matters, keeping a dedicated voice model and reading its tokens is the safer first step. Inference The design-space choice is in the design space.

Serving cost is evaluations per chunk times sessions per GPU. In Meta's account a teacher needs 120 evaluations per chunk (40 steps, three guided passes each) and the distilled student needs 2, a 60-fold cut. Four-bit quantisation (storing weights in 4 bits) trained into the model raised capacity eightfold over a two-step baseline, to 12 concurrent sessions on one GB200 GPU. Their delay is about 870 ms from the end of the user's turn to the first byte of synchronised voice and video, at 448 by 768 pixels and 25 frames per second. Divide GPU cost by concurrent sessions and you have a cost per minute, which is the figure to ask Synthesia about. Session length is the other limit: Google's Live Avatar variants support a few minutes of continuous interaction, not hours.

Two integration points. Synthesia's Interactive Avatar API layers the avatar on top of a LiveKit agent, so customers bring their own LLM and speech-to-text, and optionally text-to-speech. Its post gives no latency figure, and a fully managed stack was announced for 1 October 2026. Inference With a voice the platform does not control, renderer state must be inferred from the audio alone. Sync works the same way as in the target design: the shared timestamp clock from the target design lets video blocks align to whole 80 ms audio frames.

Consent gate. Synthesia requires a live consent video, accepts no uploads, and matches the person to the photo. It states that it strictly prohibits cloning voices without explicit consent, and moderates content at creation. Inference Make identity and consent a hard pipeline stage rather than a UI check, and bind the voice store to the same verified identity (background in Safety and provenance).

What goes wrong

  • Extra hop plus render latency. Rendering the first video block adds an illustrative 150 to 250 ms on top of the voice path. The sum can pass one second if the filler is missing or the renderer is not causal. Quote every latency with its clock: one report counts from the end of the user's turn to the first synchronised byte, another counts model time and adds network separately.
  • Sync drift. Audio and video clocks drift apart over long sessions. Timestamp every chunk, jitter-buffer on the client, and drop video before audio.
  • A blank face over an expressive voice. If the face is driven by audio alone, it lacks the intent the LLM knew, so pass style and state to the renderer.
  • Causal students degrade. A strictly unidirectional student can give rigid full-body dynamics, and multimodal conditioning can cause flicker and black frames.
  • Selling a Level 1 avatar as a conversation. A talk-only avatar looks frozen while the user speaks; listening is its own model problem and its own evaluation.
In the interview, say:

"I would drive the avatar from a unified speech LLM that owns listening, turn-taking and speaking, with a causal few-step renderer on the same audio clock and a consent gate in front of the identity and voice stores. The cascade is the fallback and baseline. Today's interactive avatars only talk, so the next step is a face that listens, and I would budget cost as evaluations per chunk and sessions per GPU."
