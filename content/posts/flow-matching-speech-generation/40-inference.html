Inference: Solving the ODE

To get speech you integrate \( \mathrm{d}x_t/\mathrm{d}t = v_\theta(x_t, t, c) \) from Gaussian noise at \( t = 0 \) to a mel spectrogram at \( t = 1 \). Every solver step is a full network pass over the whole utterance, so the cost of inference is the number of function evaluations (NFE), multiplied by the guidance passes. A common recipe in recent open and published systems is Euler at roughly 10 to 32 steps, a warped time grid and batched guidance passes, with a vocoder that becomes the bottleneck once the flow model is cheap.

Solvers and NFE

The simplest solver is first-order Euler: one network evaluation per step, moving \( x_t \) along the guided velocity \( \hat v \) (defined in the next subsection):

\[ x_{t + \Delta t} = x_t + \Delta t\, \hat v. \]

If the path from noise to data were perfectly straight, a single Euler step would be exact. Flow-matching paths are straighter than diffusion paths but not straight (From Diffusion to Flow Matching), so Euler needs a handful of steps. Higher-order solvers such as midpoint and Heun-3 spend two and three evaluations per step to cancel the curvature. Most recent flow decoders (CosyVoice 2, F5-TTS, ZipVoice) run Euler anyway, at roughly 10 to 32 steps. The extra spend rarely pays, and the schedule matters more than the solver.

Solver Cost per step When it pays off
Euler One evaluation The default; pair it with a warped schedule.
Midpoint Two evaluations When the step count is capped but wall-clock time is not.
Heun-3 Three evaluations Rarely; a small WER gain for a large time cost.

In one widely used zero-shot model with a warped schedule, doubling Euler from 16 to 32 steps lowered WER from 2.53 to 2.42 and doubled real-time factor, while Heun-3 at 16 steps reached 2.39 at nearly three times Euler's cost [3]. The same step count hides very different compute across solvers.

Quality degrades slowly as steps drop, which is why teams keep pushing the count down. A small acoustic model loses little going from 10 steps to 2 (WER 2.09 to 2.34) [7]. The trap is that WER flatters low-step samples, which are easier for ASR to transcribe, and what they lose is diversity, which WER does not measure.

The cost of guidance

Most zero-shot flow decoders run classifier-free guidance at every step. Each step evaluates the network twice, once with the text and prompt \( c \) and once with the null condition \( \varnothing \), and extrapolates away from the unconditional prediction:

\[ \hat v = v_\theta(x_t, t, c) + w\,\big(v_\theta(x_t, t, c) - v_\theta(x_t, t, \varnothing)\big). \]

Here \( w \) is the guidance strength: \( w = 0 \) is the plain conditional model, and a strength of 2 puts a weight of 3 on the conditional velocity. The derivation is in From Diffusion to Flow Matching.

The price is a doubled network cost per step. A common implementation batches the two passes into one call, as in the diagram. That saves a sequential launch but not the doubled FLOPs and activation memory, so it helps only when the GPU has headroom. Papers differ on whether NFE counts solver steps or network calls, but a guided sampler at 10 Euler steps runs 20 network passes however a paper counts.

Guidance and step count are not independent knobs. In one large zero-shot model, at 4 steps or fewer, weaker guidance (\( w = 0 \) or 0.3) gave higher speaker similarity, while from 8 steps up stronger guidance helped [1]. That makes an easy regression in speed-up work: cut the steps on a model tuned at 32 and forget to retune \( w \). The usual fix is to remove the second pass by folding guidance into a distilled model, covered in Making It Fast and Streaming.

Time-step schedules

The solver decides how each step is taken; the schedule decides where the steps go. A uniform grid spends as many steps near the data as near the noise. Every schedule in common use moves steps toward \( t = 0 \), the noise end, and does it at inference only, with no retraining.

Sway Sampling warps a uniform grid \( u \in [0, 1] \) with a single coefficient \( s \):

\[ t = u + s\Big(\cos\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}u\big) - 1 + u\Big). \]

With \( s = 0 \) the grid stays uniform, and negative \( s \) packs steps at small \( t \). The usual setting, \( s = -1 \), reduces to the cosine schedule \( t = 1 - \cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}u) \) that CosyVoice 2 ships. With the midpoint solver at 32 steps on LibriSpeech-PC, it lowered WER from 2.84 to 2.41 at zero extra cost [3]. Without the schedule, Euler degrades; the two ship together.

Pruning goes one step further. Empirically Pruned Step Sampling (EPSS) keeps the dense early steps and drops late ones. At 7 steps it reached WER 2.45, against 4.16 for an unscheduled 7-step run [8]. The floor is hard: below about 6 steps quality falls sharply, and only a model trained to take big steps goes further.

The vocoder

The ODE ends at a mel spectrogram, and a separately trained vocoder turns it into a waveform. It sits outside every NFE number above, yet it can move quality more than the solver does. In the same model, swapping the default vocoder for BigVGAN lowered WER from 2.42 to 2.11 at 32 steps [3].

Streaming systems budget the vocoder as its own term in first-packet latency, alongside the language model and the flow decoder. Its right context costs time too: in one production system the vocoder's look-ahead alone adds 130 ms [9]. Once distillation cuts the flow model to 2 to 4 steps, my read is that the vocoder and its look-ahead become the larger cost, because the flow cost shrank and the vocoder cost did not. Making It Fast and Streaming picks up from there.
