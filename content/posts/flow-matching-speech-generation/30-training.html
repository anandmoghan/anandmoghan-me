Training a Flow Matching TTS Model

The objective in the previous section turns noise into samples. TTS needs specific words, in a specific voice, over a specific number of frames. The default recipe, which came from Voicebox, is to hide most of an utterance and train the flow to fill it back in, given the text. What separates shipped systems is what the flow generates, how the text reaches it, and who decides the length.

What to generate

Most shipped flow decoders still generate a log mel spectrogram and hand it to a separately trained vocoder; CosyVoice's decoder writes a mel for HiFi-GAN. Continuous latents from an audio codec show up when the model must also render music and sound effects, and in at least one large production stack.

The mel choice makes the vocoder a separate project with its own ceiling: Voicebox's alone took 7.5 days on 8 GPUs [1]. The price, as I see it, is that the flow can never repair what the vocoder smears.

Masked infilling and prompting

Each training example hides one contiguous span covering 70 to 100% of the target mel \( x_1 \). A binary frame mask \( m \) zeroes those frames to give the context \( x_{ctx} = (1-m) \odot x_1 \). With \( x_0 \) as Gaussian noise, the path and target are the straight ones from the previous section, with \( \sigma_{\min} \) a small constant:

\[ x_t = \psi_t(x_0) = \big(1 - (1 - \sigma_{\min})\,t\big)\,x_0 + t\,x_1 \] \[ u_t = x_1 - (1 - \sigma_{\min})\,x_0 \]

The network predicts a velocity from the noisy mel, the context, the text \( c \) and the flow step. In Voicebox the loss is graded only on masked frames:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\, m,\, x_0,\, x_1} \Big[ \big\lVert m \odot \big( u_t - v_\theta(x_t, x_{ctx}, c, t) \big) \big\rVert^2 \Big] \]

The model sees every frame but is graded only on the frames it had to invent. It pays for itself: in a reduced Voicebox setup, masking the loss gave a clear gain in speaker similarity at almost no cost in intelligibility [1]. My read is that scoring visible frames rewards copying, which is not the skill you need at inference.

Prompting falls out for free, and that is a large part of why the recipe spread. At inference you lay down a reference clip, zeros for the speech to generate, and the text "reference transcript, target text". The voice is the visible context, so there is no speaker encoder to train, and the 70 to 100% mask matches that shape: a short prompt, then a long blank.

The catch is that the prompt transfers everything at once: voice, style and recording conditions come as a bundle. I would expect a reverberant prompt to come back as reverberant output. When the prompt is instead a separate input, the opposite failure appears: unless the loss excludes the prompt segment, the model learns to copy-paste it.

Text conditioning and alignment

The hard part of TTS is deciding which frames say which sounds. There are three places to put that decision, and each moves the cost somewhere else.

Text condition Who aligns What you gain What you pay
Phones expanded to frame level An external forced aligner Fast convergence A phonemizer and aligner pipeline that misses context-dependent pronunciation
Characters padded with filler tokens The flow network, implicitly No aligner, no phonemizer, no phone durations Slow convergence and a prompt-to-target boundary the model must find
Semantic tokens from an AR model The AR model Content and length fixed before the flow runs A second, autoregressive model to train and serve

Large production stacks such as Seed-TTS and the CosyVoice family take the third row. An AR model writes semantic tokens, and a diffusion or flow decoder adds the acoustic detail.

The character route pays in convergence. In one comparison, the aligned model was already intelligible at 10% of training, while the character model needed significantly more training before it finished ahead [6]. A group reproducing a character model on small Mandarin models saw a stubborn share of test samples fail outright throughout training, and re-ranking could not fix them [3]. They attribute it to entangled semantic and acoustic features.

The token route has its own leak. CosyVoice 2 removed the speaker embedding from its language model because that vector carried language and paralinguistic information that hurt prosody naturalness and cross-lingual ability. My read: a conditioning vector is rarely only the thing you named it.

Duration

A non-autoregressive model must know how many frames to generate before it starts. The older answer is a separate per-phone duration model. Newer character-based recipes predict only the total length, often with an estimate from the character counts of the prompt and target text. In AR-plus-flow stacks the question disappears, because the tokens already fix the length.

Tradeoff: one total length doubles as a speech-rate control; one model scaled it from 0.7 to 1.3 with only a moderate WER increase [6]. The cost is that the network must place every phone itself, with no timing hint beyond the total.

Time sampling and guidance dropout

Speech flow models almost all sample \( t \sim \mathcal{U}[0, 1] \) in training, not the logit-normal sampling image models use. The non-uniformity goes into the inference schedule, which can change without retraining.

Classifier-free guidance needs the same network to work without its condition, so training drops the conditions at random, about 20% of the time in most recipes. What you drop decides what you can guide. Dropping text and audio together gives one guidance direction for everything. My read is that adding a separate drop of only the audio context also teaches the model a text-only mode. With \( v_c \) and \( v_u \) the conditional and unconditional velocities, the two common ways of writing guidance, \( (1+w)v_c - w v_u \) and \( v_c + w(v_c - v_u) \), are algebraically the same, so strengths read on one scale. Guidance doubles the cost per step; see the inference section.

Architectures and scale

The open zero-shot recipes (Voicebox, E2 TTS, F5-TTS) are Transformers of about 330M parameters, trained on tens of thousands of hours. Newer ones use a DiT that injects the flow step through adaLN. Streaming decoders such as CosyVoice 2's train one network under several attention masks, from full to chunk-causal, so the same weights serve offline and streaming.

The pieces are coupled. In one set of ablations, a plain adaLN DiT without the convolutional text branch failed to learn alignment from padded characters [3]. An MMDiT "learned fast and collapsed fast" into repeated utterances with wild timbre and prosody.

Scale is less of a barrier than the big models suggest. The same group trained 158M models stably on LibriTTS (585 hours) and on single-speaker LJSpeech (24 hours), learning alignment from characters with no phonemizer [3].
