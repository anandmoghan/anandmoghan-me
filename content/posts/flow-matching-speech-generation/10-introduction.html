Introduction

Codec-token language models made zero-shot voice cloning work, but they decode token by token, and in Voicebox's comparison they lost on word error rate. Voicebox showed the alternative: generate a continuous mel spectrogram for the whole utterance in parallel, trained by masked infilling, and came out well ahead of VALL-E on word error rate [1]. Since then flow matching has become the workhorse acoustic generator, either on its own, as in F5-TTS, or behind an autoregressive token model, as in CosyVoice 2. A third pattern skips speech tokens altogether: the LLM's hidden state conditions a small diffusion head that generates each continuous latent frame, as in VibeVoice. The catch is that every solver step is a full forward pass of a large network, so the real game is keeping quality while cutting steps.

This post goes from diffusion to flow matching, then through how these models are trained, sampled and made fast enough to stream. It ends with the papers that matter, where they still break, and what to use.
