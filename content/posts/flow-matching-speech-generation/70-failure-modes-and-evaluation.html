Failure Modes and Evaluation

Most zero-shot TTS teams report the same two numbers, and at the top of the field neither separates a good system from a very good one. The failures that reach users (skipped words, odd pacing, flat delivery, degraded output from a noisy prompt) barely move a corpus-level average.

How these models are scored

The standard harness is cheap and automatic. WER comes from transcribing the generated audio with an off-the-shelf ASR model, Whisper for English and Paraformer for Mandarin. SIM is the cosine similarity between speaker-verification embeddings of the prompt and the output, usually from a WavLM-based encoder. Most labs run it on a LibriSpeech-PC test-clean subset and on the English and Mandarin sets in ByteDance's seed-tts-eval repository, then add a small listening test for naturalness.

The harness saturates first. On the seed-tts-eval English set, F5-TTS at 32 NFE scores 1.83 WER against 2.06 for the ground-truth recordings [3]. A model is not more intelligible than a person; my read is that it produces cleaner, more canonical speech that the ASR model finds easier to transcribe. At that level WER measures fit to the evaluator more than quality, and a few tenths of a point between two systems tells you little about which one listeners prefer.

The evaluator is also part of the result. If you rerank samples with one ASR model and score with another, the winner changes with the ASR family, and a same-family pair flatters itself. SIM scores from different speaker encoders do not compare, so a SIM number only means something next to a baseline scored with the same encoder. Predicted MOS models such as UTMOS and DNSMOS catch audible defects but stop agreeing with listeners once both clips are clean, which is exactly where a mature system lives. In practice that means holding the ASR model and speaker encoder fixed across every comparison, and keeping human listening tests for the decisions that matter.

Where they break

Most failures trace back to one root. The model discovers text alignment and total duration implicitly, must find where the prompt ends on its own, and inherits everything in the prompt.

Failure What you see Cause What teams do about it
Skipping and repetition Dropped words on runs of repeated words; repeated utterances Weak implicit text alignment Refine text features before alignment; train on skip and repeat augmentations
Speaking rate Mismatched pacing F5-TTS-style models set total length from the text's character count Fine-tune a duration predictor with RL
Flat prosody Correct words, monotone delivery RL on error-rate rewards (shown on non-flow TTS) Preference optimisation on human-labelled pairs
Text versus speaker Raising the text branch trades off speaker similarity One guidance weight shared by text and speaker conditioning Reweight text, speaker and joint guidance terms separately
Noisy prompts Degraded zero-shot quality Noise in the prompt hurts a model conditioned on it Noise-robust pre-training and fine-tuning, which beat cleaning the prompt first; a control to keep or remove background

Skipping and repetition shows up in training before it shows up in production, so watch per-sample WER, not just the mean. In F5-TTS's reproduction of E2 TTS, about 7% of Mandarin test samples failed with WER above 50% during training, and re-ranking could not rescue them [3]. The F5-TTS authors also found that an MMDiT variant collapsed into repeated utterances [3].

Post-training with rewards

Because WER and SIM are cheap to compute, a common recent practice is to turn them into rewards and post-train with RL. The catch is that a deterministic ODE sampler gives no likelihood for a policy gradient. One route turns the ODE into an SDE so GRPO has a likelihood to work with. The other leaves the flow decoder alone and puts the reward where likelihoods are natural: a differentiable reward on the speech-token language model, or RL on the duration predictor alone. The token-level route pays off: CosyVoice 3's 0.5B model drops Mandarin CER on seed-tts-eval from 1.16 to 0.75 [18].

Reward hacking

The reward is an ASR model or a quality predictor, and the policy learns to satisfy it rather than the listener. CosyVoice 3 already shows the cost, with speaker similarity dropping slightly after RL. A single quality predictor as the reward makes human ratings worse, while an equal-weighted ensemble of predictors resists it.

Prosody is where this bites hardest. GRPO on error-rate and likelihood rewards lowered error rates but made speech flat, in a study on a non-flow TTS model. A few hundred human preference pairs per round of iterative DPO recovered the naturalness. There is no verifiable reward for prosody, so the policy trades it away for what is measured.

Safety

A model that clones a voice from a few seconds of audio is a spoofing tool by construction, and shipped systems use two defences. The first is an audio watermark trained jointly with a detector, which can localise the mark down to individual samples. It is accurate: Audiobox reports a false positive rate of 0.001 and a worst-case false negative rate of 0.003 across its augmentations [17].

A watermark only protects outputs of a model that applies it, and anyone running released weights can skip that step. That is the case for the second defence, keeping weights in-house: ByteDance releases seed-tts-eval but withholds the Seed-TTS model "due to considerations for AI safety". Tradeoff: one of the most-reported benchmarks comes from a model outsiders cannot run.
