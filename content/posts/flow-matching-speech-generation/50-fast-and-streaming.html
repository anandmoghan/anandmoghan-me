Making It Fast and Streaming

A better time-step schedule gets a flow-matching TTS model to about 7 NFE, and then quality "sharply declines below 6 NFE" [8]. Past that point the network itself has to change. Streaming is a separate problem. A non-causal decoder that attends over the whole utterance cannot start until the text is finished, however few steps it takes. Open streaming decoders such as CosyVoice 2 and StreamFlow run about 10 guided steps. The fastest pair a 2-step decoder with block-wise causal attention and reach a first packet in the low hundreds of milliseconds.

Few-step generation

The learned velocity field curves even though each training path was straight, so one big Euler step lands in the wrong place (From Diffusion to Flow Matching). Every few-step method is a way to get a model that can take that big step anyway. In practice that means distillation: you train a normal multi-step teacher, then a 1 to 4 step student from it.

Technique How it removes steps What it costs When to use it
Reflow Retrains on pairs generated by the previous model, so paths straighten Each round needs a sampling pass with a trained model to build the pairs When you can afford repeated training rounds
Consistency Maps any point on a trajectory straight to its endpoint, for one step In the one speech result, still below the teacher When one step matters more than the last bit of quality
Mean flow Predicts the average velocity over an interval, not the instantaneous one The exact objective needs Jacobian-vector products: memory and instability When you want one model that runs at 1 to 3 steps
Distribution matching distillation Matches the student's output distribution to the teacher's, plus a GAN loss Training also needs a discriminator; metric losses added on top can game the evaluator When a 2 to 4 step student has to hold teacher quality
Teacher-free distribution matching Trains the few-step model directly against the data distribution One recent result, on single-speaker LJSpeech Worth trying when you cannot afford a teacher

A 4-step student runs about 4.5 times faster than a 16-step teacher [10], and about 14 times faster than a 128-step teacher [11]. In another case a 4-step student matches its 32-step teacher while running about 5 times faster [12].

I would expect a student trained only to match the teacher's velocity to blur at low NFE, because regressing onto an average gives an averaged mel. The students that hold quality add a signal at the level of distributions, metrics or a discriminator, which is why distribution matching with a GAN loss is a common choice. Treat a metric loss with care: a student trained with a CTC loss scored a lower WER than the ground-truth recordings [11]. Ground truth is not a perfect floor, but beating it is a warning sign that the student is partly optimising the evaluator.

Folding guidance into the model

Classifier-free guidance doubles every step, and step distillation alone does not remove it. My read is that a guided 2-NFE student still makes four model calls, half of them for the unconditional pass.

The fix is to fold guidance into the student, so it produces the guided output in one pass. The student learns to reproduce the guided teacher directly, so the unconditional branch disappears at inference. The best version conditions the student on the guidance strength as an input, so one network covers a range of strengths.

The cost is the knob. In Voicebox, the best guidance strength depends on NFE: at 4 steps or fewer, weaker guidance gives higher speaker similarity. Baking in one strength fixes the text-versus-speaker balance (Failure Modes and Evaluation) for every prompt. Keeping the strength as an input keeps both the single pass and the knob.

Streaming flow matching

Streaming needs a chunk-aware causal mask: the decoder sees past frames plus a bounded look-ahead of future frames, and renders one chunk at a time. The look-ahead gives each chunk some right context at its boundary, from a right-padded convolution or from the first few tokens of the next chunk. Chunks of about a second with a few tokens of look-ahead are typical, and some systems crossfade chunk edges.

You do not need two models for streaming and offline use. CosyVoice 2, a widely used AR-plus-flow streaming system, trains one decoder by sampling one of four masks per example: non-causal, full-causal, chunk-\( M \) and chunk-\( 2M \). It writes the first-packet latency as \( M \) tokens passing through each stage [13]:

\[ L_{\text{TTS}} = M\,d_{\text{lm}} + M\,d_{\text{fm}} + M\,d_{\text{voc}} \]

Here \( d_{\text{lm}} \), \( d_{\text{fm}} \) and \( d_{\text{voc}} \) are the per-token times of the LM, the flow decoder and the vocoder. Every term scales with \( M \), so chunk size is the latency dial. The formula leaves out two waits: the look-ahead frames, and the text tokens the LM buffers before it emits its first speech token. Every frame of look-ahead is paid once, in first-packet time, which is why production systems keep it to a few tokens.

Serving under load

Distrust real-time factor (RTF) comparisons across papers. The same open model at 32 NFE is reported at RTF 0.31, 0.30 and 0.17 on three different GPUs [3, 10, 12]. Implementation matters as much as hardware: on the same GPU class, another paper measures that model at 0.123 against 0.31 [8]. Only ratios measured on one machine mean anything: in one, a 2-NFE streaming system against CosyVoice 2 at 10 NFE cut first packet from 843 ms to 377 ms on one RTX 4090 [14].

Concurrency changes the ranking. In one system that ships both decoders at 0.6B parameters, the DiT has the better RTF at one stream (0.234 against 0.288), but at six streams it reaches 0.709 against 0.434 for the causal decoder [9]. Its first packet grows from 138 ms to 481 ms, while the causal decoder's grows from 97 ms to 299 ms. A single-stream benchmark would pick the wrong decoder for a fleet, so benchmark at the concurrency you will serve.

The two decoders also differ in token rate, but my read is that part of the gap is structural. The AR stage reuses its KV cache from token to token. The flow stage cannot reuse activations across ODE steps, because its input \( x_t \) changes at every step. A bounded context window is the nearest substitute, and my read is that it also caps memory per stream.

Flow-stage cost is NFE, times one plus the guidance pass, times model size and length, so step distillation and guidance folding multiply. My read is that once the flow model is down to 2 to 4 NFE, the vocoder becomes the next bottleneck, because its cost did not shrink. In the same system, the vocoder's right-context look-ahead alone adds 130 ms [9].
