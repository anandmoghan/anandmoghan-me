Online RL for Flow-Matching TTS

Pretraining regresses a velocity field onto data, and nothing in that loss targets WER, speaker similarity or naturalness, which the evaluation harness already scores cheaply. GRPO closes that gap by scoring a group of samples per prompt and pushing toward the better ones, but it assumes a stochastic policy with a likelihood at every step, and an Euler ODE sampler has neither.

Why GRPO does not fit a flow model

For a prompt with \( G \) rollouts and scalar rewards \( R^i \), the advantage is:

\[ \hat A^{i} = \frac{R^{i} - \operatorname{mean}_{j} R^{j}}{\operatorname{std}_{j} R^{j}} \]

A rollout counts as good only if it beats its siblings, so no learned critic is needed. If every rollout scores the same, the standard deviation is zero and the group carries no signal, which is why the recipe below drops such groups. The update multiplies the advantage by a clipped ratio of new to old step likelihoods, with a KL penalty to a frozen reference. That ratio is where a flow model breaks: once the initial noise is drawn every ODE step is deterministic, so a step has no density, and the group explores only through its starting noise.

Four ways around it

The four workarounds differ in what becomes the policy. The first is to turn the ODE into an SDE for rollouts. Adding noise with a chosen rollout schedule \( \sigma_t \), and correcting the drift with a score term that for rectified flow is a function of \( v_\theta \), keeps the marginals at every \( t \):

\[ x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + \mu_\theta(x_t, t)\,\Delta t + \sigma_t \sqrt{\Delta t}\,z \]

Here \( \mu_\theta \) is the velocity plus that correction and \( z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \) is a fresh draw per step, so each step is a Gaussian with a closed-form density and GRPO applies unchanged. Rollouts dominate the cost, so they run short: for Flow-GRPO on images, cutting rollout steps from 40 to 10 trained over 4x faster with no loss in final reward [22]. The FlowTTS-GRPO recipe also injects noise only in a short window of early steps, trains only those, and drops CFG from rollouts, which converged faster and still helped with CFG back on at inference [23]. The catch is that the injected noise is itself a defect: an SDE baseline on an AR-diffusion TTS model lost UTMOS on average [24].

The second retrains the network to output a Gaussian over the velocity, so every step has a density without changing the sampler. It works, but F5R-TTS needed a 1M-update pretraining run before its 1,100 GRPO updates [25].

The third skips the likelihood. Roll out with the ordinary ODE sampler, take each rollout's final latent \( x_1^{i} \), and re-noise it along the straight path with \( x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \) and \( \sigma_{\min} = 0 \):

\[ x_t^{i} = (1 - t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1^{i} \]

The flow matching loss is then weighted by the group advantage \( \hat A^{i} \), with a second term that anchors the velocity to the frozen reference velocity \( v_{\text{ref}} \) at weight \( \beta \):

\[ \mathcal{L} = \sum_{i} \Big[ \hat A^{i} \big\| v_\theta(x_t^{i}, t) - (x_1^{i} - x_0) \big\|^2 + \beta \big\| v_\theta(x_t^{i}, t) - v_{\text{ref}}(x_t^{i}, t) \big\|^2 \Big] \]

The advantages have zero mean, so the loss pulls toward above-average rollouts and away from the rest. The sign matters: WER and SIM rewards cluster tightly within a group, so positive-only weights mostly teach the model to imitate its own samples.

The fourth leaves the flow model alone and runs RL where likelihoods already exist: the speech-token LM in two-stage systems, or a categorical duration predictor in F5-TTS-style models. Industry has used this route most: Seed-TTS ran REINFORCE on SIM and WER rewards [15], and CosyVoice 3 backpropagated a differentiable reward into its tokens [18]. In those systems the LM sets intelligibility; when CosyVoice 3's flow stage was trained with GRPO, its ASR reward stalled near 0.99 while similarity kept climbing [23]. The reverse holds too: a flow decoder and vocoder trained on clean audio partly undo an LM-only MOS reward, and speaker attributes respond weakly because the speaker enters at the flow stage.

A working recipe

Start from a converged checkpoint, which doubles as the frozen reference, and build prompts as (prompt audio, prompt text, new target text) triples so RL sees the zero-shot task. Sample 8 to 16 rollouts per prompt: for Flow-GRPO on images, groups of 6 and 12 collapsed where 24 held [22], and a duration policy was unstable at 8 but not at 16 [12]. Score each rollout with a fixed ASR model (one minus WER or CER), a speaker-verification cosine to the prompt and optionally a MOS predictor. Normalise each reward by its batch standard deviation before summing so the noisiest one does not dominate; FlowTTS-GRPO weights similarity and ASR at 1.0 and DNSMOS at 0.4 [23]. Drop groups whose rewards barely differ. Train a LoRA adapter (rank 32 is under 3% of CosyVoice 3's flow model [23]) with a KL or velocity anchor, for hundreds to about ten thousand updates against hundreds of thousands to a million in pretraining.

Reward hacking

The policy optimises the reward models, not the listener. Seed-TTS lowered WER by speaking slowly and over-articulating, at a cost in naturalness [15]. Learned reward models get gamed by acoustic artifacts, much as a single MOS predictor does in the previous section. Training length is its own risk: the DMOSpeech 2 duration policy degraded sharply after about 1.5K to 2K steps [12].

I would use three defences. Sum several rewards and keep the KL anchor on; on images, KL and early stopping were not interchangeable. Evaluate with judges the policy never saw, a different ASR model and speaker encoder than the rewards use; in FlowTTS-GRPO, WavLM similarity, not a reward, still rose from 0.777 to 0.804 on seed-tts-eval Mandarin [23]. Stop on a held-out listening set that tracks speaking rate, not on training reward.
