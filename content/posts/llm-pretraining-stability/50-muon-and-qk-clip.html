Muon and QK-Clip

For years the optimizer was the one part of the recipe nobody touched. Muon changed that: Kimi K2 and DeepSeek-V4 both pretrain most of their weights with it. It brought its own instability, exploding attention logits, and its own fix.

What Muon changes

AdamW scales every coordinate on its own. Muon treats each 2D hidden-layer weight as a matrix. It keeps a momentum buffer of the gradients \( G_t \), then replaces that buffer by the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix before applying it:

\[ M_t = \mu M_{t-1} + G_t \] \[ O_t = \operatorname{NS}(M_t) \]

With the singular value decomposition \( M_t = U \Sigma V^\top \), the result is \( O_t \approx U V^\top \): every singular value set to about one. An SVD every step is too slow, so Muon runs a few Newton-Schulz iterations from \( X_0 = M_t / \lVert M_t \rVert_F \) [21]. Each is an odd polynomial with tuned constants \( a, b, c \) that pushes the singular values towards one and stays stable in bf16:

\[ X_{k+1} = a X_k + b \,(X_k X_k^\top)\, X_k + c \,(X_k X_k^\top)^2 X_k \]

Moonshot's version then rescales the update to match AdamW's typical update RMS and adds decoupled weight decay, for an \( n \times m \) weight [2]:

\[ W_t = W_{t-1} - \eta \big( 0.2 \sqrt{\max(n, m)}\; O_t + \lambda W_{t-1} \big) \]

Both additions were needed to make it scale. Without weight decay, weights and layer outputs kept growing past the range where bf16 is precise; with the RMS match, Muon reuses AdamW's learning rate and decay [21]. Embeddings, the output head and the norm weights stay on AdamW. The compute is cheap: one momentum buffer instead of two, and optimizer time of 1 to 3% of the forward-backward pass [21]. The cost is engineering, because orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix and a sharded optimizer has to gather each weight before it can step.

Logit explosion and QK-Clip

In a 9B-active, 53B-total MoE trained with plain Muon, Moonshot saw the maximum attention logit pass 1,000, a level that in their experience brings loss spikes and occasional divergence [2]. Their hypothesis is spectral. An Adam update is dominated by a few large singular values, while a Muon update has all of them equal. A full-rank update has more chances to line up with the existing singular directions of \( W_q \) or \( W_k \) and grow them. The logit is bilinear in the two, so any growth compounds.

The usual fixes did not fit. Soft-capping acts after the dot product has already grown, and QK-norm needs materialised keys, which MLA avoids at inference (see Fixes in the architecture). QK-Clip instead edits the weights. For head \( h \), let \( Q_i^h \) and \( K_j^h \) be the query and key of tokens \( i \) and \( j \) in a training sequence \( X \) from batch \( B \), with head dimension \( d \). QK-Clip records the largest pre-softmax logit over the batch, and when it exceeds a threshold \( \tau \) it shrinks the head's query and key projections after the optimizer step:

\[ S_{\max}^h = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \max_{X \in B} \max_{i, j} Q_i^h K_j^{h\top} \] \[ \gamma_h = \min\big(1, \tau / S_{\max}^h\big) \] \[ W_q^h \leftarrow \gamma_h^{\alpha} \, W_q^h \] \[ W_k^h \leftarrow \gamma_h^{1 - \alpha} \, W_k^h \]

With \( \alpha = 0.5 \) the shrink splits evenly between queries and keys. Under MLA the per-head non-rotary (content) query and key components each take \( \sqrt{\gamma_h} \) and the per-head rotary query takes \( \gamma_h \). The shared rotary key is left alone, so one head's clip does not leak into the others. Kimi K2 used \( \tau = 100 \). Max logits rose to the cap, then decayed below it after about 30% of training with no change to \( \tau \) [2]. In the first 70,000 steps 12.7% of heads triggered the clip at least once, and none did afterwards [2]. By Moonshot's own report, K2 then trained on 15.5T tokens with zero loss spikes [2].

Tradeoff: QK-Clip is a post-step weight edit, not a loss term. It leaves the current step's forward and backward pass untouched, so the gradient never learns that the logits are too large. It also needs a per-head statistic out of the attention kernel every step. In its favour, an aggressive \( \tau = 30 \) in a small MoE barely moved the loss [2].

DeepSeek-V4 shows the other route. It uses Muon for most modules, with Nesterov momentum 0.95, weight decay 0.1 and update RMS rescaled to 0.18 [17]. It skips QK-Clip because its attention applies RMSNorm directly to queries and KV entries. It still hit loss spikes, traced to outliers in its MoE layers rather than attention (see Low precision and mixture-of-experts). Normalising queries and keys bounds the logits; it does not bound everything Muon can grow.

How much Muon actually buys

Moonshot's headline is about twice the compute efficiency of AdamW in compute-optimal training, matching it with roughly 52% of the FLOPs [21]. An independent comparison of ten optimizers from 0.1B to 1.2B parameters, at up to 8x the Chinchilla token ratio, tells a narrower story [22]. Many claimed speedups come from weak baselines: tuning only AdamW's learning rate in the GPT-3 recipe was worth nearly 2x on its own for a model of about 100M parameters [22]. Against a properly tuned AdamW, matrix-based optimizers like Muon still win, but the margin shrinks steadily as models grow and is small at the largest size tested. Rankings also flip during the learning-rate decay, so a comparison read mid-run can point the wrong way.

My read is that labs adopt Muon for token efficiency at a fixed data budget, which is how the Kimi K2 report frames it [2], and accept a new failure mode with it. If you switch, budget for the distributed implementation, watch per-head max logits from the first step, and tune the AdamW baseline before you believe the gain.
