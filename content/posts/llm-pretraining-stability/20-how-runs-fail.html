How long runs fail

Long runs fail in two shapes. A spike is a jump in loss over a few steps that either recovers or ends in NaNs. Slow divergence is a norm that creeps up for thousands of steps until something overflows or the softmax saturates. Both get worse with scale: PaLM saw its spikes only in the 540B model, at irregular intervals and sometimes late in training [1]. Meta reproduced the pattern on GPUs with AdamW, with prominent spikes at 546B, moderate trouble at 65B and none at 30B or 7B [3].

The loss curve is usually among the last places a failure shows up. Each mechanism below has a signal that moves first, so it pays to log those signals per layer and not just watch the loss.

Attention logit growth

Attention logits \( q \cdot k / \sqrt{d} \) can grow without bound. As they do, the softmax collapses to a near one-hot distribution with almost zero entropy, and training diverges. ViT-22B hit this after a few thousand steps at around 8B parameters, and layer norm on the queries and keys fixed it [4]. The growth comes from the query and key norms rising, not from better alignment, and a logit depends quadratically on those norms [5].

The signal is the maximum attention logit per layer, with per-head attention entropy as a second view. It is predictable enough to extrapolate: every point with a max logit above \( 10^4 \) diverged, and a 4.8B model predicted to diverge without QK-norm did [5]. QK-norm does not end the problem, though: sensitivity to the learning rate still rises with model size [5]. The mechanism also returns when the recipe changes; Kimi K2 saw max logits quickly pass 1000 in a mid-scale run with vanilla Muon [2].

Output logit drift

The softmax over the vocabulary only cares about differences between logits, so nothing stops all of them from drifting together. Late in training the output logits wander away from log probabilities and become very negative, which a z-loss on \( \log Z \) prevents in small models [5]. Chameleon hit a multimodal version: modalities sharing weights competed by growing their norms until they left the bf16 range, as late as 20 to 30 percent into training [6].

The signal is the norm of the last layer's output, whose growth Chameleon found strongly predicted later divergence [6]. OPT-175B saw related warnings: final-layer activation norms spiking and the dynamic loss scale crashing to zero [7]. Marin's 8B run skipped z-loss, and its output head (lm_head) blew up only in the deep cooldown at the end [8]. The failure mode is timing: Chameleon's drift appeared as late as 20 to 30 percent in [6], and Marin's only in the cooldown [8], so a short ablation may never reach the point where it breaks.

When Adam's statistics go stale

AdamW scales each update by a running estimate of the squared gradient:

\[ u_t = \frac{m_t}{\sqrt{v_t} + \epsilon} \]

If the gradient grows faster than \( v_t \) can track, the denominator is too small and the update too large. Loss spikes tend to follow such an underestimate by one to eight iterations, and they grow with model size, batch size and learning rate [9]. Lowering \( \beta_2 \) from the PyTorch default of 0.999 shortens that memory and mitigates them, though going too low slows training [9].

A subtler version starts with gradients that are too small. Meta's analysis of its large runs proposes a chain: early-layer gradients fall below \( \epsilon \), their updates vanish, and the large batch makes consecutive gradients strongly correlated in time [3]. When a rare batch pushes those gradients back above \( \epsilon \), the ratio \( m_t / \sqrt{v_t} \) goes bimodal near \( \pm 1 \) for many entries at once, and the update stops pointing downhill [3]. In their runs the early-layer gradient norm exploded one step before perplexity did [3]. The same chain explains why most spikes heal on their own: once gradients lose their time correlation, the update shrinks back. The signals are per-layer gradient norms and the shape of \( m_t / \sqrt{v_t} \); in my experience a single global norm tells you something moved, not where.

Data and model state together

The obvious suspect is bad data, and it is usually only half the story. PaLM replayed the batches around each spike from an earlier checkpoint and saw no spike, and concluded that spikes come from specific batches meeting a specific parameter state [1]. OLMo found long repeated n-grams in many spike batches, but the same sequence could spike a large model and not a small one, and masking such sequences reduced gradient spikes without removing them [10]. On the model side, GLM-130B found embedding gradients orders of magnitude larger than other layers early on, with collapses trailing gradient-norm spikes by a few steps [11].

That interaction is why the classic recovery works and why it is a trap. Rolling back and skipping batches puts the run in a different state with different data, so the spike usually does not recur there. It also throws away the evidence: you never learn which mechanism fired, and the cause stays in the recipe. Skipping helps mainly early in training, and spikes get more frequent later [3]. Scale brings them back too: Marin's 32B run spiked far more than its 8B run, and clipping and step skipping softened those spikes without removing them [12]. Only switching the architecture to QK-norm mid-run did. My read is that rollback buys time rather than fixing anything: before restarting, pull the per-layer gradient norms, max logits and output norms around the spike and decide which mechanism fired.
