Running a long job

Past a few thousand accelerators, a pretraining run stops being a modelling problem and becomes an operations problem. The fixes above lower the odds of a spike, but somebody still has to see one coming, decide whether to rewind, and keep the job alive through constant hardware faults.

What to watch

The training loss is the worst early warning on the dashboard: by the time it moves, the damage is done. The signals that move first are cheap to log, and each maps to a failure mechanism from earlier in the post.

Signal Failure it catches Evidence
Global gradient norm Spikes from any cause, a few steps early In GLM-130B, collapses trailed gradient-norm spikes by a few steps [11]. OLMo 2 counts values at least seven standard deviations from a 1,000-step rolling average [10].
Per-layer gradient norm Adam updates gone stale in one group of layers In a 546B run, early-layer gradient norm exploded one step before the perplexity spike [3]
Per-layer update size against epsilon Layers that quietly stop learning Once gradient RMS nears the AdamW epsilon, updates shrink and parameters stop moving [3] [5]
Max attention logit per layer or head Attention logit growth In small-scale proxy runs, every model whose max logit passed about \( 10^4 \) diverged, and the trend across sizes predicted it [5]
Final-layer output norm Output logit drift Output-norm growth predicted later divergence in Chameleon's mixed-modal training [6]
Dynamic loss scale Numerical overflow in loss-scaled mixed precision OPT-175B's divergences came with the loss scale crashing to zero [7]

The global signals are the cheapest to log, but only the per-layer and per-head ones point at a cause.

Rollback, skip and data filters

The classic response is the one PaLM documented. Its 540B run spiked about 20 times despite clipping at 1.0, and each time the team restarted from a checkpoint about 100 steps before the spike and skipped roughly 200 to 500 batches [1]. Replaying the same batches from an earlier checkpoint did not spike, so the cause was those batches meeting that parameter state. Skipping is only well defined because PaLM's data order was deterministic: each batch depended only on the step number.

The data side can be cleaned ahead of time. OLMo 2 found that spike batches often held long runs of repeated n-grams, so it removes documents with 32 or more repeated n-grams of 1 to 13 tokens and masks such spans in the loss [10]. That reduced gradient spikes without eliminating them.

Every skip and rollback costs tokens, compute and determinism, and the final model ends up trained on a data stream nobody planned. Rollback also stops working as a run ages: the Adam-instability analysis found that batch skipping helps mainly early, and spikes grow more frequent later [3]. DeepSeek-V4 automated spike detection and short rollbacks, found that rollbacks alone did not stop the spikes recurring, and added a routing fix that costs about 20% wall-clock when always on, so it runs only after a detected spike [17].

Marin 32B is the clearest public account of the operational fixes running out. The Llama-style 32B run spiked far more than its 8B sibling, so the team tightened clipping from 1.0 to 0.2, added update-norm clipping and step skipping, rebuilt the optimizer state and tried Muon. In their words, clipping and step-skipping "softened the spikes but never removed them" [12]. What worked was an architecture change mid-run: they warm-started the step-80,000 weights into the Qwen3 32B layout, which adds QK-norm, with a 1,000-step re-warmup. The switch cost a one-time loss penalty that recovered in about 10B tokens, and the spikes disappeared entirely [12]. The lesson I take from it is that persistent spikes are a design bug, and run-time heuristics only buy time to fix it.

Hardware faults and silent corruption

At frontier scale, hardware interrupts the run far more often than the optimizer does. In a 54-day window of Llama 3 405B training on 16,384 H100s, the job was interrupted 466 times, 419 of them unexpectedly, and about 78% of the unexpected ones were confirmed or suspected hardware faults [18]. Six were silent data corruption. Meta still kept effective training time above 90%, with manual intervention only three times.

Silent data corruption is the dangerous case, because the job keeps running on wrong numbers. Google expected it to hit Gemini training "every week or two", and handled it with deterministic replay to isolate bad computations, scanners on idle machines and hot standbys [30]. Redundant in-memory copies of the model state let a job recover from an intact replica instead of persistent storage, which raised goodput on the largest job from 85% to 97% [30].

Determinism, spare capacity and scanners all cost throughput. The payoff goes beyond uptime: I would expect a corrupted reduction to look exactly like an optimization spike on the loss curve, and only a replay tells the two apart. Llama 3 found several of its numerical issues the same way, by comparing loss across parallelism setups [18].
