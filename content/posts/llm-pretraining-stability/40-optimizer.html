Optimizer settings that keep AdamW steady

Most large dense and MoE runs still train with AdamW, and the settings have converged. DeepSeek-V3 is a typical example: \( \beta_1 = 0.9 \), \( \beta_2 = 0.95 \), weight decay 0.1 and a global gradient-norm clip of 1.0 [16]. The \( \beta_2 \), weight decay and clip are not the PyTorch defaults.

Beta2, epsilon and update clipping

The PyTorch default \( \beta_2 = 0.999 \) averages squared gradients over roughly the last thousand steps. When the gradient scale changes faster than that, the second moment goes stale and underestimates \( g_t^2 \). The next updates then come out too large (the mechanism is in How long runs fail). Wortsman et al. saw loss spikes follow this underestimate within one to eight iterations, and the spikes grew with model size, batch size and learning rate [9]. Lowering \( \beta_2 \) shortens the memory and mitigates them. The cost is on the other side of the sweep: push it much lower and training slows down.

Update clipping treats the same failure without touching \( \beta_2 \). It tracks, per tensor, how far the squared gradient sits from its running estimate \( u_t \), and scales the learning rate down when the estimate is out of date:

\[ \mathrm{RMS}_t = \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\big[ g_t^2 / u_t \big]} \] \[ \eta_t' = \frac{\eta_t}{\max(1, \mathrm{RMS}_t)} \]

This is Adafactor's trick, ported to AdamW as StableAdamW, and it beat gradient clipping on CLIP ViT-Huge [9]. It costs one extra reduction per tensor, but production recipes use a lower \( \beta_2 \) plus a global clip instead.

Epsilon fails in the opposite direction. Gradient RMS shrinks as models and learning rates grow. In the small-scale proxy study it fell close to the default \( \epsilon = 10^{-8} \) at the largest settings, so updates shrank and parameters stopped learning [5]. Dropping \( \epsilon \) to \( 10^{-15} \) on a 4.8B model fixed it, and raising it to \( 10^{-6} \) caused an instability [5]. OLMo 2 moved from \( 10^{-5} \) (a common LM-codebase default) to \( 10^{-8} \) and saw the gradient norm settle faster and stay lower [10]. DeepSeek-V4 goes all the way to \( 10^{-20} \) for its AdamW parameters [17].

Tradeoff: a small epsilon only means something if the optimizer state can represent numbers that small. Molybog et al. list lower epsilon as a mitigation and flag in the same breath that it conflicts with low-precision arithmetic [3]. fp16 cannot hold \( 10^{-8} \) at all, and the squared gradient of a \( 10^{-5} \) gradient is far below its range. That is why the moments live in fp32 or bf16, which keeps fp32's exponent range; DeepSeek-V3 stores them in bf16 [16].

Warmup, clipping and weight decay

Warmup ramps the learning rate up to its peak instead of starting there. Longer warmup lowers learning-rate sensitivity, and more so for larger models [5]. The lengths in recent reports look short in steps, from 8,000 steps for Llama 3 405B [18] to 2,000 for DeepSeek-V3 [16] and 500 for Kimi K2 [2]. With batches of tens of millions of tokens, a few thousand steps already span many billions of tokens. Warmup does not fix a stale second moment, though; Wortsman et al. saw the high-\( \beta_2 \) spikes even with 5,000 warmup steps [9].

Gradient clipping at a global norm of 1.0 is the cheap safety net against a single outlier batch. It is not a stability fix, and the record shows it. PaLM spiked about 20 times with clipping at 1.0 [1]. OPT-175B lowered the threshold to 0.3 early in the run [7]. Marin 32B went to 0.2 and added update-norm clipping and step skipping, which in the team's words softened the spikes but never removed them [12]. The structural causes (stale moments, growing attention logits) sit inside a step that the clip only shortens. My read is that a low threshold also fails quietly: once most steps are clipped, the clip acts as a learning-rate cut that no schedule plot shows.

Weight decay at 0.1, decoupled from the Adam preconditioner, is shared by DeepSeek-V3, Kimi K2 and DeepSeek-V4 [16, 2, 17]. It is a stability knob as much as a regulariser: in the proxy study, output-logit divergence appeared at every scale without it [5]. Its scope matters as much as its value. OLMo 2 removed decay from the token embeddings because it shrank them, and small embeddings inflate early-layer gradients through the norm that follows [10]. Fixes in the architecture covers the embedding side.

Transferring hyperparameters across scale

Nobody sweeps the learning rate at 400B parameters. The settings for the final run come from small runs, and the question is whether the optimum at 100M parameters is anywhere near the optimum at 100B. Often it is not: the largest learning rate that trains stably shrinks as models grow, and adding depth raises sensitivity faster than adding width [5]. A value that was safe in the proxy can diverge at scale.

muP (maximal update parameterisation) rescales initialisation and per-layer learning rates with width so the optimum stays put. Tensor Programs V tuned a 40M-parameter proxy, transferred the settings to a 6.7B model, and spent 7% of the pretraining cost on tuning [19]. MiniCPM's "wind tunnel" runs found the best base learning rate near 0.01 across 0.04B to 2.1B parameters [20]. muP also lowers learning-rate sensitivity in the small-scale proxy study [5].

Tradeoff: muP lowers the risk that the big run's learning rate is wrong, but few frontier labs say they use it in the final run. One likely reason is scope: muP's guarantee covers width at a fixed recipe, while a frontier run changes things the proxy never saw (depth, batch-size ramps, MoE routing, long-horizon weight decay). And a parameterisation bug is silent; the run trains, just not at the optimum you paid to find.
