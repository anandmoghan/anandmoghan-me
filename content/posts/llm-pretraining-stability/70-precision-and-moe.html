Low precision and mixture-of-experts

The dense bf16 recipe is well understood; the two things labs layer on top of it are not. Lower precision saves memory and bandwidth but makes every large value expensive, and sparse experts add a router that can collapse or blow up on its own. The fixes have the same flavour in both cases: scale in finer groups, keep a few sensitive operations in high precision, and control the router directly.

From fp16 to FP8

fp16 training needed loss scaling because its narrow exponent range let small gradients underflow. bf16 keeps fp32's 8 exponent bits and drops to 7 mantissa bits, so it needs no loss scaling, and it became the default. The price moved from range to rounding: bf16's roundoff error can be up to 65,536 times worse than fp32's at large magnitudes [25]. That is why the accumulating paths stayed wide: Llama 3 405B accumulates gradients across micro-batches in fp32 and reduce-scatters them in fp32 across data-parallel workers [18].

FP8 pushes the same trade one step further. The standard recipe uses E4M3 for weights and activations, E5M2 for gradients, and delayed scaling, which picks each tensor's scale from earlier iterations' maximum values. Delayed scaling needs no extra pass, but it assumes the statistics are stable from step to step, and a single outlier breaks that assumption [26].

DeepSeek-V3 is the clearest FP8 recipe at frontier scale. It scales activations per 1×128 tile (one token, 128 channels) and weights per 128×128 block, and computes each scale online from the current maximum [16]. It also found that FP8 tensor-core accumulation on the H800 keeps only about 14 bits, so it promotes partial sums to fp32 registers on the CUDA cores every 128 elements [16]. The embedding, output head, MoE gating, norms and attention stay in bf16 or fp32, as do master weights and gradients. The full 14.8T-token run had no irrecoverable loss spikes and no rollbacks. The trade-off is kernel complexity: per-tile scales mean custom GEMMs, dequantisation inside the matmul, and an accumulation workaround the paper itself calls a hardware deficiency.

Outliers and FP4

Low-precision failures are mostly outlier failures. A block's scale is set by its largest value, so one huge activation squeezes everything else in the block into a few representable levels. Finer groups contain the damage, which is why the block sizes keep shrinking.

The outliers do not have to be present at the start. Intel trained Llama 2 7B in FP8 and saw a sharp loss divergence after about 220B tokens; the outliers only appeared after roughly 200B [26]. The cause was SwiGLU: it multiplies two linear branches, and as their weights slowly align, the product grows much faster than either branch. Their fix, Smooth-SwiGLU, rescales channels before quantisation without changing the function, and trained a 7B model to 2T tokens in FP8 with up to about 34% more throughput [26]. The practical lesson is that an FP8 run validated over a few hundred billion tokens has not been validated against this failure.

NVIDIA's NVFP4 recipe trained a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer on 10T tokens in 4-bit [27]. It stores E2M1 values in blocks of 16 with an E4M3 scale per block and an fp32 scale per tensor. On top of that it applies 16×16 random Hadamard transforms to the weight-gradient inputs to spread outliers, uses 2D block scaling for weights and stochastic rounding for gradients. It also keeps bf16 islands: the first 2 and last 8 blocks, 16% of the linear layers, plus attention, softmax, embeddings, the head and norms [27]. The gap to FP8 widened as the learning rate decayed, and switching to higher precision for the decay closed much of it.

Those islands are the trade-off. Every layer left in bf16 gives back throughput, and the list of layers was picked by hand for one model, on hardware only Blackwell has. I have not seen a lab publish a frontier model pretrained in FP4.

Routers and load balancing

A mixture-of-experts router is a softmax (or sigmoid) over expert logits, and it inherits every logit problem from the earlier sections. ST-MoE ran the router in fp32 and still saw instability at its largest scale [25]. In its example, at a logit of 128.5, bf16 roundoff of 0.5 changes the softmax output by 36% [25]. The fix was the router z-loss, the mean squared log-sum-exp of the router logits over a batch of \( B \) tokens and \( N \) experts:

\[ L_z = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} \Big( \log \sum_{j=1}^{N} e^{x_j^{(i)}} \Big)^2 \]

It keeps logits small, so the exponential sees small rounding errors, and ST-MoE used a coefficient of 0.001 [25]. In their comparison it stabilised training without hurting quality, while clipping the updates also stabilised it but cost a lot of quality.

The other MoE failure is imbalance. When a few experts take most tokens, routing collapses and expert parallelism stalls on the hot devices. The usual cure is an auxiliary balance loss, and DeepSeek states the cost plainly: too large a balance loss hurts model quality. Their alternative is auxiliary-loss-free balancing. Each expert gets a bias that is added to its affinity score for top-K selection only; the gate weight still uses the original score. After every step the bias of an overloaded expert drops by \( \gamma \) and an underloaded one rises by \( \gamma \), with \( \gamma = 0.001 \) for the first 14.3T tokens and zero for the last 500B [16]. A tiny sequence-wise balance loss stays on to stop extreme imbalance within a single sequence.

Qwen3 went the other way and kept a balance loss, but computes it over the global batch [28]. The argument for doing so is that balancing per micro-batch forces every sequence, including narrow-domain ones, to use all experts evenly, which works against the specialisation an MoE is supposed to learn [29]. Neither choice is free. A loss trades quality for balance through its coefficient, and the bias method replaces that coefficient with a per-step load statistic and a tuned update speed that the lab has to decide when to switch off.
