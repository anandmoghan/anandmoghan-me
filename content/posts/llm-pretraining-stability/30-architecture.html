Fixes in the architecture

Two mechanisms from the previous section cause most divergences: attention logits that grow until softmax goes one-hot, and output logits that drift until the final normaliser leaves a safe range. The fixes labs ship today are small edits to the transformer block, each aimed at one of them. Each has a cost in an unexpected place: kernels, inference caches or the residual stream.

QK-norm and the end of soft-capping

QK-norm applies an RMSNorm (or LayerNorm) to queries and keys before the dot product, so a logit can no longer grow just because the projection weights did. It came from ViT-22B, where an 8B-parameter model diverged on near-one-hot attention. Small-scale proxy runs made the case for language models: without it the largest stable learning rate shrinks as models grow, and with it a 1.2B model trained at a learning rate of 0.3 [5].

\[ s_{ij} = \frac{\operatorname{RMSNorm}(q_i)^\top \operatorname{RMSNorm}(k_j)}{\sqrt{d}} \]

It is close to default now: OLMo 2, Qwen3 and Gemma 3 all ship it. It narrows the problem rather than ending it. Learning-rate sensitivity still rises with scale when QK-norm is in place, and in OLMo 2's ablations it gave poor results on its own, helping only together with the reordered norm below.

Gemma 2 took the other route, soft-capping: squash each logit \( s \) (an attention score or a final-layer logit) through a scaled tanh, with \( c = 50 \) for attention and \( c = 30 \) for the final layer [13]. Gemma 3 replaced it with QK-norm [14].

\[ s \leftarrow c \tanh(s / c) \]

Moonshot's argument against capping is that it acts after the dot product, so query and key norms keep growing underneath a cap that hides them. The practical objection is kernels. A cap is a nonlinearity inside the attention scores, so every fused kernel in training and serving must implement it; FlashAttention added softcapping only in version 2.6, for Gemma 2 and Grok. QK-norm runs before the kernel, so stock kernels work unchanged. My read is that this weighed as much as the stability argument.

QK-norm has its own constraint. Multi-head latent attention (MLA), the DeepSeek design that Kimi K2 also uses, caches a compressed latent and never materialises full keys at inference, so there is no key to normalise. Moonshot built QK-Clip instead, a per-head rescale of the query and key weights after each optimizer step; Muon and QK-Clip covers it.

Z-loss on the output

Normalised queries and keys do nothing for the last softmax. Softmax is unchanged when you add a constant to every logit, so the loss does not pin where the logits sit, and their normaliser can wander until it leaves the range low-precision formats handle well. Chameleon needed a fix for this on top of QK-norm. The fix, from PaLM, is an auxiliary z-loss that pulls \( \log Z \) toward zero, with \( \alpha = 10^{-4} \) [1]:

\[ \mathcal{L}_z = \alpha \log^2 Z \] \[ Z = \sum_{i=1}^{V} \exp(z_i) \]

Here \( z_i \) are the output logits over a vocabulary of \( V \) tokens. It is one extra term on logits you already compute; Chameleon used \( 10^{-5} \), and OLMo 2 adopted it too. With QK-norm and z-loss both in place, the small-scale proxy runs [5] found that changing warmup, weight decay or μP mostly moved learning-rate sensitivity, not stability.

The trap is the implementation. OLMo 2 found that FlashAttention's fused cross-entropy with z-loss matched a plain PyTorch version in the forward pass but not in the backward pass, which they suspected was a precision difference. They abandoned the fused version and retrained from the point of divergence. The lesson I take from it: pick one loss implementation before launch and do not swap it mid-run, because a kernel you checked only in the forward pass is not equivalent yet.

Where the norms go

Pre-norm, normalising each sub-layer's input, became the GPT-style default because the original post-norm layout trains badly when stacked deep. It leaves the sub-layer output unnormalised, and activations can grow with depth; Chameleon traced that growth to the feed-forward block, which SwiGLU's multiplicative gate makes worse.

Two answers are in use. OLMo 2 moves the norm to each sub-layer's output, before the residual add, and drops the input norm (the reordered norm). Gemma 2 and 3 keep the input norm and add the output one, a sandwich. Qwen3 stays with plain pre-norm plus QK-norm. In OLMo 2's ablation, reordered norm and QK-norm together cut the gradient spike score from 0.108 to 0.069 [10].

\[ h = x + \operatorname{RMSNorm}(\operatorname{Attn}(x)) \]

The cost is a different residual stream. Under pre-norm each sub-layer adds whatever magnitude it produces; under a post-sublayer norm every add has a scale set by the norm's gain weights, so those gains now govern how the stream grows with depth. My read is that this is why the layout does not drop into an existing recipe: Chameleon found reordering worked poorly with dropout and dropped dropout from its 34B, and OLMo 2 changed its initialisation in the same release. Sandwich norm is no universal fix either; it did not stabilise GLM-130B's test runs, and in Qwen's gated-attention study it restored convergence at higher learning rates while barely improving the loss.

Embeddings and initialisation

A lot of early instability starts in the embedding layer. GLM-130B saw embedding gradients several orders of magnitude above other layers early in training, with gradient-norm spikes a few steps before each collapse. Its fix shrinks the gradient into the embedding \( e \) and leaves the forward pass alone, with \( \gamma = 0.1 \) and \( \operatorname{sg} \) a stop-gradient [11]:

\[ e \leftarrow \gamma\, e + (1 - \gamma) \operatorname{sg}(e) \]

It removed most spikes, though the final run still had three late divergences, handled by shrinking further.

Stable pre-norm training needs embeddings with a standard deviation near 1 and small sub-layer weights; PaLM initialises embeddings from \( \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \) because no norm follows them. OLMo 2 adds a quieter fix, no weight decay on embeddings. Decay shrinks them, and small embeddings followed by a norm produce large gradients in the early layers.

OLMo 2 also replaced its depth-scaled initialisation with a single \( \mathcal{N}(0, 0.02) \) for every parameter. Under a deliberately short warmup meant to provoke spikes, the gradient spike score fell from 0.40 to 0.03 [10]. The price was slightly slower convergence, and a short-warmup stress test says little about a normal warmup.

Gated attention

The newest change is a head-specific sigmoid gate after scaled dot-product attention, before the output projection. Qwen's study compared 30 variants on 15B MoE and 1.7B dense models over 3.5T tokens; this placement worked best, and the authors say it "nearly eliminates loss spikes" and tolerates larger learning rates and batches.

\[ o = \operatorname{Attn}(q, k, v) \odot \sigma(x W_g) \]

The mechanism runs through attention sinks. A softmax head must put its weight somewhere, so heads with nothing to add park it on the first token: in the baseline, 46.7% of attention went there on average [15]. The gate lets a head output close to zero instead, and the gated models show no sink and fewer massive activations.

The cost is small: under 2% latency, with the FFN narrowed to keep the parameter count matched. The evidence is thinner than the claim: the loss-spike comparison is one pair of 1.7B runs. I would treat the gate as an addition to QK-norm and z-loss, not a replacement.
