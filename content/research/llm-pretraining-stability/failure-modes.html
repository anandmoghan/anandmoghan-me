LLM pretraining stability: failure modes

Research note, 2026-10-14. Claims carry a source and a grounding tag.

Scope and grounding

  • Covers what loss spikes and divergence look like, their proposed mechanisms, how teams detect them, and the classic operational responses. inference
  • Papers were downloaded from arXiv and read as full text for the sections quoted below. The OPT-175B logbook PDF and the BLOOM chronicles were not read in full: OPT claims come from the OPT paper, and BLOOM chronicle claims come from a page summary. inference
  • Tag meaning: confirmed = primary source text read; reported = secondary or summarised source; inference = my own reasoning.

What spikes and divergence look like in big runs

  • PaLM 540B: loss spiked roughly 20 times despite gradient clipping (global norm 1.0). The spikes came at highly irregular intervals, sometimes late in training, and did not appear in the smaller models. Source: PaLM: Scaling Language Modeling with Pathways, arXiv:2204.02311, section 5.1. confirmed
  • PaLM response: restart from a checkpoint roughly 100 steps before the spike and skip roughly 200 to 500 data batches. The loss did not spike again at the same point. Source: arXiv:2204.02311, section 5.1. confirmed
  • PaLM did not blame bad data: re-training on the same batches from an earlier checkpoint did not spike. The authors conclude spikes come from the combination of specific batches with a particular parameter state. Source: arXiv:2204.02311, section 5.1. confirmed
  • PaLM also used an auxiliary z-loss of 1e-4 times log^2 Z to keep the softmax normaliser near 0, which the authors say increased stability. Source: arXiv:2204.02311, section 5. confirmed
  • Meta replicated PaLM-style spikes on GPUs with a different codebase, data and AdamW, at 7B, 30B, 65B and 546B. Severity grew with size: 546B had prominent spikes, 65B moderate instability, and 30B and 7B no explosive divergence. Source: A Theory on Adam Instability in Large-Scale Machine Learning (Molybog et al.), arXiv:2304.09871, introduction. confirmed
  • In the Meta 546B run most spikes recovered on their own within about a dozen iterations. Only the harder ones needed batch skipping. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, introduction. confirmed
  • OPT-175B: loss divergences were fixed by lowering the learning rate and restarting from an earlier checkpoint. Restart points were chosen where the dynamic loss scalar was still healthy (at least 1.0) and activation norms trended down afterwards. Source: OPT: Open Pre-trained Transformer Language Models, arXiv:2205.01068, training processes section. confirmed
  • OPT-175B correlated divergence with the dynamic loss scalar crashing to 0 and the L2 norm of final-layer activations spiking. Source: arXiv:2205.01068. confirmed
  • OPT-175B other mid-flight changes: gradient clip lowered from 1.0 to 0.3 early on; a switch to vanilla SGD (plateaued, reverted to AdamW); resetting the loss scalar (fixed some, not all); a newer Megatron version that reduced pressure on activation norms. Source: arXiv:2205.01068. confirmed
  • OPT-175B dropped some Pile subsets because they raised instability risk, measured as gradient-norm spikes at 1.3B scale. Source: arXiv:2205.01068, data section. confirmed
  • OPT-175B also suffered at least 35 manual restarts from hardware failures, over 100 hosts cycled in 2 months, and an estimated 70+ automatic restarts. Hardware restarts are a separate issue from divergence but share the rollback machinery. Source: arXiv:2205.01068. confirmed
  • GLM-130B: precision-related loss spikes were frequent and became more frequent as training went on. Some recovered on their own; others were preceded by a sudden rise in gradient norm and ended in a spike or NaN. Source: GLM-130B: An Open Bilingual Pre-trained Model, arXiv:2210.02414, section 2.3. confirmed
  • GLM-130B states OPT-175B handled spikes by manually skipping data and adjusting hyperparameters, and BLOOM by an embedding norm. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, section 2.3. confirmed
  • Gopher: the Adafactor optimiser showed more instability than Adam at scale. A 7.1B Adafactor run at half the peak learning rate of the Adam run (6e-5 versus 1.2e-4) still showed instabilities. Source: Scaling Language Models: Methods, Analysis and Insights from Training Gopher, arXiv:2112.11446, appendix C.1. confirmed
  • BLOOM 104B preliminary runs in float16 had numerical instabilities causing irreversible divergence. The authors hypothesise float16 overflow; final training in bfloat16 mixed precision solved it. Source: BLOOM: A 176B-Parameter Open-Access Multilingual Language Model, arXiv:2211.05100, section 3.4.3. confirmed
  • BLOOM 176B had only one loss spike, from which it recovered swiftly, so little human intervention was needed. Source: arXiv:2211.05100, section 3.4. confirmed
  • BLOOM chronicles: the single spike was around iteration 31219 (loss 5.098, grad norm 960.351) and recovered in about 30 iterations. The 176B run passed the 24B-token point where the 104B run had failed. The author lists clean data, bf16, FP32 gradient accumulation, lower init and embedding layer norm as a mix with no ablations. Source: bigscience-workshop/bigscience, train/tr11-176B-ml/chronicles.md (page summary). reported
  • Llama 3 405B: batch size started at 4M tokens and doubled twice, to 16M tokens after 2.87T tokens, "to improve training stability" early on. The report says it saw few loss spikes and needed no interventions for divergence. Source: The Llama 3 Herd of Models, arXiv:2407.21783, section 3.3. confirmed
  • Llama 3 405B interruptions: 466 in a 54-day window, 47 planned and 419 unexpected; about 78% of unexpected ones were confirmed or suspected hardware, GPU issues alone 58.7%; manual intervention was needed only three times. Source: arXiv:2407.21783, section 3.3.4. confirmed
  • Llama 3 fixed several numerical issues by comparing loss across parallelism setups, using FP32 gradient accumulation and FP32 reduce-scatter of gradients. Source: arXiv:2407.21783, section 3.3. confirmed
  • Llama 3 vision adapters: bf16 gradient accumulation caused numerical instabilities after the image encoder was added, so they moved to FP32 accumulation. Source: arXiv:2407.21783, vision experiments section. confirmed
  • Llama 3 reports that in interruption terms instability was dominated by hardware, not optimisation, at 16K GPU scale. That contrast with PaLM, OPT and GLM is my reading of the report. inference

Mechanisms

Attention logit growth and entropy collapse

  • Attention logits z = q.k / sqrt(d) grow large, softmax weights collapse to near one-hot (low entropy), and training diverges. Named attention logit growth by Dehghani et al. and attention entropy collapse by Zhai et al. Source: Wortsman et al., Small-scale proxies for large-scale Transformer training instabilities, arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.1.1. confirmed
  • In ViT-22B, divergent loss appeared after a few thousand steps at around 8B parameters. The cause was extremely large attention logits giving almost one-hot attention weights with near-zero entropy; LayerNorm on queries and keys fixed it. Source: Scaling Vision Transformers to 22 Billion Parameters, arXiv:2302.05442, section 2. confirmed
  • Zhai et al. track per-head attention entropy as a sharpness proxy and find low entropy coincides with oscillating or diverging loss. They prove a lower bound on entropy that decays exponentially with the spectral norm of the attention logits, and propose sigma-Reparam. Source: Stabilizing Transformer Training by Preventing Attention Entropy Collapse, arXiv:2303.06296 (abstract page). reported
  • Wortsman et al. find the logit growth is due to growth of the queries and keys, not better alignment between them. Their hypothesis is the quadratic dependence of attention logits on parameter norms. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.1.1. confirmed
  • Without qk-layernorm the maximum learning rate at which models train stably shrinks with model size. With qk-layernorm a 1.2B model trains at learning rate 0.3. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.1.1. confirmed
  • Predictable: tracking the max attention logit across scales and fitting a quadratic, they saw every point above 1e4 diverge and predicted a 4.8B model without qk-layernorm would diverge at LR 1e-2. It did. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.3. confirmed
  • Transplanting scaled attention logits into a 10M model, loss degraded around a logit scale of 1e3 and beat a bigram model only below 1e4. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.3. confirmed
  • GLM-130B also saw attention scores grow past the FP16 range as the model scaled. PB-Relax did not help there; BLOOM sidestepped it with bf16. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, section 2.3. confirmed

Output logit divergence and norm drift

  • Output logits diverge from log probabilities and become very negative, late in training. The PaLM z-loss (log^2 Z, coefficient 1e-4) resolves it in small models. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.1.2. confirmed
  • In Wortsman et al. the instability appeared at any scale without weight decay, and weight decay also mitigated it for the larger models they tested. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.1.2. confirmed
  • Chameleon: standard Llama architecture diverged from slow norm growth in mid-to-late training. The explanation is softmax translation invariance, softmax(z) = softmax(z + c): with shared weights, modalities compete by growing norms until bf16 range is exceeded. Divergence appeared as late as 20 to 30 percent into training. Source: Chameleon: Mixed-Modal Early-Fusion Foundation Models, arXiv:2405.09818, section 2.3. confirmed
  • Chameleon calls this the logit drift problem (after Wortsman et al.) and says monitoring output-norm growth strongly correlates with predicting later divergence. Source: arXiv:2405.09818, section 2.3. confirmed
  • Chameleon fixes: QK-norm (7B diverged after about 20 percent of an epoch without it), dropout 0.1 for 7B, norm re-ordering (Swin style) for 34B, and z-loss. Source: arXiv:2405.09818, section 2.3. confirmed

Adam second moment, epsilon and time-correlated updates

  • Molybog et al.: Adam can enter a state where the update vector has large norm and is essentially uncorrelated with the descent direction, leading to divergence. More likely in deep models with large batch sizes. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, abstract. confirmed
  • Proposed sequence: gradients of a group of parameters (most likely earlier layers) vanish below epsilon, so the Adam moments and updates vanish. Parameters barely move and the large batch makes consecutive gradient estimates strongly correlated in time. Meanwhile other layers keep changing until a rare batch makes the early-layer gradient exceed epsilon. The update ratio m/sqrt(v) then becomes large and bimodal near +/-1 for many entries, and a chain reaction follows. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 6. confirmed
  • In their runs, the gradient norm of earlier layers exploded one training step before the perplexity spike. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 6. confirmed
  • The theory also explains self-recovery (the gradient loses time correlation) and why the same data does not spike from another state. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, sections 1 and 6. confirmed
  • The authors attribute the nature of the problem to Adam itself, but note that testing a different optimiser such as SGD in the same setup is still needed to confirm. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, introduction. confirmed
  • In smaller-scale experiments they saw that dataset composition changes how many layers have gradients diminishing to zero. For natural language this happened with a high-quality corpus of lower variety of text modalities. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 7.1. confirmed
  • Mitigations they discuss: skipping batches (works mainly early in training, needs manual tracking, spikes get more frequent later as gradients drop below epsilon); lower learning rate; lower epsilon (conflicts with low-precision arithmetic); smaller batch size; lower beta1 and beta2 (stale updates); setting epsilon to 0 and mapping updates to 0 when v is 0; monitoring a unimodality statistic of m/sqrt(v) and resetting optimizer state or lowering epsilon when it turns bimodal. The authors conclude there is no silver bullet. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 7.1 and 9. confirmed
  • Wortsman et al. observe gradient RMS shrinks with both model size and learning rate until it nears the default AdamW epsilon of 1e-8 at their largest setting. The AdamW update then shrinks and parameters stop receiving learning signal. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.4. confirmed
  • Lowering epsilon to 1e-15 for a 4.8B model at LR 0.3 improved loss and prevented the gradient RMS collapse; raising it to 1e-6 caused an instability. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.4. confirmed
  • Interpretation: Molybog et al. and Wortsman et al. both point to gradients falling to the epsilon scale as a risk, but describe opposite end states: spike versus stalled learning. inference

Embedding and LayerNorm instabilities

  • GLM-130B: the embedding layer's gradient norm was often several orders of magnitude larger than other layers early on. A collapse usually trailed a gradient-norm spike by a few steps. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, section 2.3. confirmed
  • GLM-130B embedding gradient shrink: word_embedding = word_embedding * a + word_embedding.detach() * (1 - a), with a = 0.1, removed most spikes at negligible latency. The final run had only three late-stage divergences, further handled by shrinking more. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, section 2.3. confirmed
  • GLM-130B: Pre-LN let main-branch value scale grow extremely large in deep layers; Pre-LN and Sandwich-LN both crashed, and DeepNorm-initialised Post-LN was the stable choice. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, sections 2.1 and 2.3 and appendix. confirmed
  • BLOOM added a LayerNorm directly after the embedding (from bitsandbytes StableEmbedding), which significantly improved stability in 104B tests. It also penalised zero-shot generalisation in earlier work, and the authors suggest bf16 might remove the need for it. Source: arXiv:2211.05100, section 3.2. confirmed
  • OLMo 2 excluded token embeddings from weight decay. Weight decay on embeddings shrank their norm, and small embeddings give large gradients in early layers because the LayerNorm Jacobian grows when its input is small. Source: 2 OLMo 2 Furious, arXiv:2501.00656, section 3.4.2. confirmed
  • Chameleon's norm re-ordering bounds the norm growth of the SwiGLU feed-forward block, which is multiplicative and can become problematic. Source: arXiv:2405.09818, section 2.3. confirmed
  • Spike No More: spikes are linked to sudden gradient-norm growth; analysis of sub-layer Jacobian spectral norms says stability needs small sub-layers and a large shortcut path. Source: Spike No More: Stabilizing the Pre-training of Large Language Models, arXiv:2312.16903 (abstract page). reported

Massive activations and outlier features

  • LLMs contain a handful of activations far larger than others (e.g. 100,000 times the median), numbering fewer than 10 among tens of millions, with values above 15,000 in LLaMA2-70B. They sit in fixed feature dimensions at specific tokens (start token, first period or newline) and stay roughly constant regardless of input. Source: Massive Activations in Large Language Models, arXiv:2402.17762, abstract and introduction. confirmed
  • They act as fixed bias terms and concentrate attention on those tokens, an implicit attention bias. Source: arXiv:2402.17762. confirmed
  • This paper studies trained models, not the dynamics of divergence. Linking massive activations to loss spikes in pretraining is my own connection and is not stated by the source. inference
  • GLM-130B notes outlier dimensions (about 0.1 percent of feature dimensions, per Dettmers et al.) in the context of quantisation, not training stability. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, quantisation discussion. confirmed

Data, initialisation and slow gradient-norm growth

  • OLMo-0424 had frequent loss spikes, often preceded by gradient-norm spikes, and a slow growth in gradient norm that correlated with spike frequency. Larger models spiked more often. Source: arXiv:2501.00656, section 3. confirmed
  • Spike batches often contained long repeated n-gram sequences, but the link is not deterministic: the same sequence may spike a large model and not a small one, or not after reshuffling. Masking loss on such sequences mitigated gradient spikes without removing them and did not change the slow norm growth. Source: arXiv:2501.00656, section 3.1. confirmed
  • OLMo 2 stability recipe: repeated-n-gram filtering, plain initialisation instead of scaled, QK-norm, reordered norm, z-loss, no weight decay on embeddings. Source: arXiv:2501.00656, section 3. confirmed
  • OLMo 2 test of initialisation with a short warmup: the new init had no loss spikes and the gradient-norm spike score fell from 0.40 to 0.03. Source: arXiv:2501.00656, section 3.2. confirmed

Small-scale proxies

  • Wortsman et al. reproduce attention logit growth and output logit divergence in small models at high learning rates, and mitigations used at scale work equally in this regime. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, abstract. confirmed
  • Metric: LR sensitivity, the expected deviation of final loss from optimal when varying learning rate across three orders of magnitude. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 2.2. confirmed
  • qk-layernorm and z-loss reduce LR sensitivity; longer warmup helps larger models; independent weight decay helps; muParam helps; scaling depth raises LR sensitivity faster than scaling width. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, sections 3.1 and 3.2. confirmed
  • Even with qk-layernorm, LR sensitivity still grows with model scale. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, figure 1. confirmed
  • Their setup: AdamW, default warmup 5e3 steps, 1e5 total steps, independent weight decay 1e-4, z-loss coefficient 1e-4. Sizes in the main plots run from 2.4M to 1.2B parameters, plus a 4.8B check. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 2. confirmed
  • OLMo 2 uses the same idea: reproduce spikes quickly at small scale by shortening warmup, then ablate fixes. Source: arXiv:2501.00656, section 3.2. confirmed
  • Molybog et al. argue the opposite caution: reproductions of spikes at smaller scale are not necessarily caused or fixed by the same factors as at large scale, so they analyse 7B to 546B runs directly. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, introduction. confirmed

How practitioners detect problems

  • Gradient norm: GLM-130B calls it an informative indicator of collapse, with the collapse lagging a gradient-norm spike by a few steps. Source: arXiv:2210.02414, section 2.3. confirmed
  • Gradient norm spikes precede loss spikes in OLMo 2 experiments, and OLMo 2 defines a spike score as the share of values at least seven standard deviations from a rolling average of the last 1,000 values. Source: arXiv:2501.00656, section 3. confirmed
  • Per-layer gradient norms: early-layer gradient norm blew up one step before perplexity in the Meta 546B run. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 6. confirmed
  • Per-layer gradient and update RMS compared with the AdamW epsilon, to see parameters stop learning. Source: arXiv:2309.14322, section 3.4. confirmed
  • Max attention logit per layer, with a fitted trend across scales as an early warning; attention entropy per head as a sharpness measure. Sources: arXiv:2309.14322; arXiv:2303.06296. confirmed
  • Output norm of the last transformer layer for logit drift (Chameleon), final-layer activation L2 norm and dynamic loss scale (OPT). Sources: arXiv:2405.09818; arXiv:2205.01068. confirmed
  • Statistic for the optimiser: the shape (uni- versus bimodal) of m/sqrt(v) over a layer. Proposed by Molybog et al. with initial experiments only. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 7.1. confirmed
  • Update-norm monitoring as such is not a named metric in any source I read; the Adam theory implies it, which is my inference. inference

Classic operational responses

  • Rollback and skip data: PaLM (about 100 steps back, skip 200 to 500 batches), Meta 546B (also skipped batches where needed). Sources: arXiv:2204.02311; arXiv:2304.09871. confirmed
  • Rollback and lower learning rate: OPT-175B, with careful choice of the restart checkpoint. Source: arXiv:2205.01068. confirmed
  • Tighter gradient clipping: OPT lowered it from 1.0 to 0.3 early on. PaLM spiked with clipping at 1.0, so clipping alone did not prevent spikes. Sources: arXiv:2205.01068; arXiv:2204.02311. confirmed
  • Optimiser state reset (loss scale reset in OPT; optimiser state reset suggested by Molybog et al.). Sources: arXiv:2205.01068; arXiv:2304.09871. confirmed
  • Preventive changes: z-loss, QK-norm, norm re-ordering, embedding gradient shrink or embedding norm, higher precision for sensitive ops (bf16 over fp16, FP32 accumulation), data filters, smaller initial batch size. Sources: arXiv:2204.02311; arXiv:2302.05442; arXiv:2405.09818; arXiv:2210.02414; arXiv:2211.05100; arXiv:2501.00656; arXiv:2407.21783. confirmed
  • Costs of the rollback approach: needs manual loss tracking, more frequent checkpoints, and lost compute; frequency of spikes can rise later in training. Source: arXiv:2304.09871, section 7.1. confirmed
  • BLOOM chronicle: checkpoints every 100 iterations after a hardware loss, about 3 hours maximum lost; each checkpoint 2.3 TB and about 40 seconds to save. Source: bigscience chronicles.md (page summary). reported

Open questions and conflicting sources

  • Root cause. Molybog et al. attribute spikes to Adam's time-correlated, epsilon-dominated updates; Wortsman, Dehghani and Zhai attribute them to attention logit growth, and others to output-logit drift or embedding gradients. These may be different failure modes or different views of one chain; no source tests them head to head. inference
  • Epsilon. Wortsman et al. report lowering epsilon to 1e-15 helps at 4.8B; Molybog et al. warn lowering epsilon conflicts with low-precision training. Wortsman et al. also find raising epsilon to 1e-6 triggers instability. Sources: arXiv:2309.14322; arXiv:2304.09871. confirmed
  • Data blame. PaLM says the spike batches are not bad data in themselves; OLMo 2 finds repeated n-grams associated with spikes and filtering helps on average, but not deterministically. Not strictly contradictory but different emphasis. Sources: arXiv:2204.02311; arXiv:2501.00656. confirmed
  • fp16 versus bf16. BLOOM and GLM-130B authors attribute many instabilities to fp16 range; GLM-130B chose to stay in FP16 for hardware portability and relied on EGS and DeepNorm instead. BLOOM bf16 explanation is a hypothesis, not an ablation. Sources: arXiv:2211.05100; arXiv:2210.02414. confirmed
  • Embedding LayerNorm. BLOOM found it helped stability but hurt zero-shot results in a separate study; whether bf16 makes it unnecessary is untested. Source: arXiv:2211.05100, section 3.2. confirmed
  • Post-LN versus Pre-LN. GLM-130B found DeepNorm Post-LN stable and Pre-LN crashing; much of the field (and Wortsman et al.) uses Pre-LN with qk-layernorm. I did not find a direct comparison across these two stances. Source: arXiv:2210.02414. inference
  • Llama 3 reports few spikes with no divergence interventions, and the section read does not credit specific stabilisers beyond small initial batch size and FP32 accumulation. Which recipe elements mattered is not stated. Source: arXiv:2407.21783, section 3.3. confirmed
  • Not verified: the OPT-175B logbook PDF and the BLOOM chronicles in full; the Spike No More and Zhai et al. details beyond the abstract pages; Gopher's own stability text beyond the Adafactor appendix. inference
  • Tied to massive activations: no source read ties massive activations to training divergence. inference
